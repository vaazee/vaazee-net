The episode's brief

The lesser-known Norman invasions

England fell in a day and was held down in five years. The lands beyond it took two centuries, were never taken whole, and were conquered by a different method. The episode's description asks three things.

Three countries, three ways in

There was never a Norman plan for the British Isles. What the three stories share is the people: the same few dozen families, and the same toolkit of knight, archer, castle and charter.

Anatomy of a castle

The English had fortified towns, built for everyone. The castle was a new thing: a private fortress for one lord and a few dozen men, cheap enough to put up in weeks and strong enough to hold a district. Tap a marker.

Question ii · 1067 to 1169

Wales, castle by castle

Ten steps across a century. Green dots are castles in Norman hands; brown dots are castles the Welsh have taken back. The dotted line is Offa's Dyke, the old frontier.

Two Waleses. By 1100 the country had split in a way that would last nearly two hundred years. Marchia Wallie, the March, was the patchwork of Norman lordships along the border and the south coast, each with its castle, its borough and its own law. Pura Wallia, Wales proper, was the mountain north and west under native princes. Edward I closed the gap in 1282 and 1283, with another ring of castles.
The bridge from Wales to Ireland

One family

The first invaders of Ireland were not a royal army. They were, almost to a man, the sons and grandsons of one Welsh princess: Nest, daughter of the last king of Deheubarth. Tap anyone on the tree.

Solid lines are parent and child. Dotted lines are Nest's partners. Her grandson Gerald of Wales wrote the history of the invasion, which is one reason the family looms so large in it.

The arithmetic of 1169 to 1171

How few they were

Gerald of Wales gives numbers for each landing. They are small enough to draw. Squares are knights, dark dots are other mounted men in mail, gold dots are Welsh archers.

How did so few do so much? Gerald's own answer was the combination: armoured horsemen for open ground, and Welsh longbowmen from Gwent to deal with lightly armed men at a distance. Irish armies of the time fought without mail, with axes, javelins and slings, and had no answer to either.

The other answer is that it was not at first a war between nations. The newcomers were one more faction in Irish politics, hired by a king of Leinster against his rivals, and many Irish kings found them useful. It was Henry II's arrival that turned a mercenary adventure into a lordship.

The long shadow

Irish popular memory counts eight hundred years from Strongbow. The line is not as straight as that suggests: the first colony shrank, went native and nearly vanished before the Tudors began again. Even so, every later chapter starts from the claim made in 1171.

Hiding in plain sight

What they left

Some of the most Irish-sounding surnames there are arrived on those ships, and the Welsh landscape is still organised around the castles of the March.

Within a few generations many of these families spoke Irish, fostered their children with Irish neighbours, kept poets and married into the old dynasties. In 1366 the Dublin government passed the Statutes of Kilkenny to forbid exactly that. A later saying held that they had become more Irish than the Irish themselves.

Scotland was never conquered by the Normans. William marched to Abernethy in 1072 and took King Malcolm III's submission, and that was the limit of it.

The change came from the top. David I, king from 1124, had grown up at the court of Henry I and held an English earldom. He brought his friends north and gave them land on feudal terms, founded burghs and abbeys, and built a kingdom on the Anglo-Norman pattern with the Scottish crown still firmly on a Scottish head.

The newcomers' descendants fought for that crown two centuries later. Both claimants in 1291 and the eventual winner in 1306 carried names from northern France.
