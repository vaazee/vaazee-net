IWhat tonal harmony is

The one paragraph that defines the whole subject, and the four properties that make Bach and a pop song members of the same family.

Harmony is the sound that results when two or more pitches are performed simultaneously. It is the vertical aspect of music, produced by combining the components of the horizontal aspect (melody and rhythm).

Much of today's popular music is based on tonal harmony, just as Bach's music was — which means the two have a good deal in common. Four properties do the work:

Property 1

A tonal center

A pitch class that provides a center of gravity — the note the music leans toward and comes to rest on. Everything else is heard in relation to it.

Property 2

Major and minor scales

Both bodies of music use these two scale families almost exclusively. Seven notes, a fixed pattern of whole and half steps.

Property 3

Tertian chords

Tertian means "built of thirds." C–E–G is tertian; C–F–B is not. Stacking thirds is the default chord-building move of the entire system.

Property 4

Function

Chords built on the various scale degrees relate to each other and to the tonal center in fairly complex ways. Each chord has a more or less standard role within a key — hence functional harmony.

The definition

Tonal harmony refers to music with a tonal center, based on major and/or minor scales, and using tertian chords that are related to one another and to the tonal center in various ways.

A pitch class is a note name regardless of octave: every C on the piano — low, middle, high — belongs to pitch class C. There are twelve pitch classes, which is why the keyboard repeats every twelve keys.

IIPitch

Scales, key signatures, and intervals — the raw material. If you only ever internalize one thing here, make it the major scale's step pattern.

The keyboard & pitch class

The smallest distance between two adjacent keys — black or white — is a half step (semitone). Two half steps make a whole step. The white keys are the letters A–G; the black keys are named relative to their neighbours, so the same black key is C♯ (C raised) or D♭ (D lowered). Those two names sound identical on the piano — they are enharmonic equivalents.

Label Click a key to hear it

Two octaves from C4 (middle C). Notice the black-key groups of two and three — that pattern is how you find any note by eye: C sits immediately left of a group of two.

Why half steps sit where they do

There is no black key between E–F or between B–C. Those two pairs are half steps on the white keys, and that single fact generates every key signature in the system.

The major scale

The major scale is a fixed pattern of whole (W) and half (H) steps:

Half steps in the major scale occur only between scale degrees 3̂ and 4̂, and between 7̂ and 1̂. Everything else is a whole step. Because the pattern is fixed, transposing it to a different starting note forces you to add sharps or flats — which is exactly what a key signature is.

Tetrachords

The major scale can be thought of as two identical four-note patterns separated by a whole step. Those four-note patterns are called tetrachords: each is W–W–H.

Why this matters

The upper tetrachord of one major scale is the lower tetrachord of the scale a fifth above it. Start on G, reuse C major's upper tetrachord (G–A–B–C), continue the pattern, and you need F♯. That is the circle of fifths in one sentence.

Key signatures

A key signature is a pattern of sharps or flats at the beginning of a staff indicating that certain notes are consistently raised or lowered. There are seven sharp signatures and seven flat signatures.

Naming rule — sharps

The name of the major key is found by going up a half step from the last sharp in the signature.

Last sharp F♯ → G major. Last sharp C♯ → D major.

Naming rule — flats

The name of the major key is the same as the next-to-last flat in the signature. The exception is F major, which has one flat.

Flats B♭ E♭ A♭ → next-to-last is E♭ → E♭ major.

Memorize these two strings

Order of sharps: F C G D A E B  ·  Order of flats: B E A D G C F — the same letters backwards. Say them aloud until they are automatic; nearly every key-signature question reduces to them.

Key

Accidentals in standard treble-clef placement. Note the pattern shifts at the fifth sharp — that is conventional, not arbitrary; it keeps the signs on the staff.

Enharmonic key pairs

Three pairs of major keys sound exactly the same — the very same keys of the piano:

B major = C♭ major

5 sharps / 7 flats

F♯ major = G♭ major

6 sharps / 6 flats

C♯ major = D♭ major

7 sharps / 5 flats

The circle of fifths

A diagram shaped like a clock face. Reading clockwise, each new key begins on 5̂ (the fifth scale degree) of the previous key and adds a sharp. Reading counterclockwise, each new key begins on 4̂ of the previous one and adds a flat. The inner ring gives the relative minor of each major key — they share a key signature.

The minor scales

Minor comes in three forms. All three share the same first five notes — the minor pentachord, W–H–W–W. Only 6̂ and 7̂ vary.

Relative and parallel keys

Minor key signatures conform to the natural minor scale, no matter which minor form is actually in use — the raised 6̂ and 7̂ appear as written accidentals in the music.

Relative keys

Share the same key signature. The relative major of any minor key starts on 3̂ of the minor scale; the relative minor of any major key begins on 6̂ of the major scale.

c minor ↔ E♭ major (3 flats).

Parallel keys

Share the same 1̂. C major and c minor are parallel keys; C major is the parallel major of c minor.

Same tonic, different signature.

Shortcut

To find any minor key signature other than g♯, d♯, or a♯: start with the key signature of the parallel major and add three flats and/or subtract three sharps. C major has 0 → c minor has 3 flats. D major has 2 sharps → d minor has 1 flat.

Convention used on this page: uppercase letters for major keys (E♭ major), lowercase for minor (c minor).

Every degree has a traditional name. These names are the vocabulary of harmonic analysis — "dominant" and "subdominant" will appear constantly from Part V onward.

The symmetry worth seeing

The mediant lies halfway between tonic and dominant; the sub-mediant lies halfway between tonic and sub-dominant. The "sub-" names are not "one below" — they are the mirror image, measured downward from the tonic. That is why 4̂ is called the subdominant even though it sits just below 5̂.

Intervals

An interval is the measurement of distance in pitch between two notes. A harmonic interval sounds both notes at once; a melodic interval sounds them one after the other.

Every interval name has two parts: a numerical name and a modifier preceding it. Count letter names inclusively — C up to E spans C, D, E, so it is a 3rd, whatever the accidentals. We say unison instead of 1 and octave (8ve) instead of 8. Intervals smaller than an octave are simple; those an octave or larger are compound (a 9th, an 11th).

Perfect, major, minor

The easiest way to learn the modifiers is to relate them to the major scale — specifically the intervals from 1̂ up to each other scale degree. Once learned, apply it in any context, whether or not a major scale is in play.

  • Perfect (P) is used only for unisons, 4ths, 5ths, octaves, and their compounds. P1, P4, P5 and P8 all appear using 1̂ of the major scale as the bottom note.
  • Major (M) and minor (m) are used only for 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths, and their compounds. The intervals 1̂–2̂, 1̂–3̂, 1̂–6̂, 1̂–7̂ in the major scale are all major intervals.
  • Make a major interval a half step smaller without changing its numerical name and it becomes minor. You can shrink an interval either by lowering the top note or raising the bottom.

Augmented and diminished

  • A perfect or major interval made a half step larger (same numerical name) becomes augmented (+ or A).
  • A perfect or minor interval made a half step smaller (same numerical name) becomes diminished (° or d).
  • There is no such thing as a diminished unison. Doubly augmented and doubly diminished intervals exist but are rare.
  • Tritone is the term for the +4 or its enharmonic equivalent, the °5 — three whole steps, the interval that splits the octave exactly in half.

Inversion of intervals

Descending intervals, especially large ones, are often easier to spell and identify through inversion: put the bottom pitch above the top one, or the reverse. D up to A inverts to A up to D.

The rule of 9

The new numerical name is always 9 minus the old one.

2↔7 · 3↔6 · 4↔5 · 1↔8

The modifier flips too

M↔m · +↔° · P stays P. A M3 inverts to a m6; a P5 inverts to a P4.

Worked example

What note lies a m6 below G3? Invert the m6 down into a M3 up: G3 up a M3 is B3. Transpose B3 down an octave and the answer is B2.

Consonance and dissonance

Roughly, consonant means pleasing to the ear and dissonant means not — but these are heavily dependent on context. Some of the most exciting moments in tonal music are dissonances, which are hardly displeasing there; they resolve eventually to the consonances that give them meaning.

Consonant

Major and minor 3rds and 6ths; perfect 5ths and octaves (and the unison).

Dissonant

All other harmonic intervals, including every augmented and diminished interval. Exception: the P4 is dissonant in tonal music only when it occurs above the lowest voice (the bass).

IIIRhythm

Rhythm is the general term for the time aspect of music, as contrasted with the pitch aspect.

Durational symbols

Durations are notated with symbols organised so that each symbol is twice the duration of the next shorter one and half the duration of the next longer one. That is the entire system — a binary tree.

Ties

A tie is a curved line connecting two notes of the same pitch, creating a new duration equal to their sum. It is how you write a duration the symbols alone can't express — and how you carry a note across a bar line.

A tie joins identical pitches; a slur connects different ones and means "play smoothly." Different things.

Dots

A dot following a symbol always adds one-half the value of the note, rest, or dot that precedes it. So a dotted half = half + quarter. A double-dotted half = half + quarter + eighth.

Notation detail: a dot is never placed on a staff line. If the notehead is on a line, the dot goes to the right of the note but in the space above it.

Beat, tempo, meter, time signatures

Beat

The basic pulse of a passage. To find it, tap your foot, or imagine the conductor's arm movement.

Tempo

The rate at which the beats occur.

Meter

Beats group into patterns consistent throughout a passage. The pattern of beats is the meter; the groups are measures (m. / mm.), separated by bar lines.

The two questions a meter answers

Meter is classified along two axes, and a time signature encodes both:

  1. How many beats per measure? Two = duple, three = triple, four = quadruple.
  2. How does the beat divide? Into two = simple, into three = compound.
TypeBeat divides intoDupleTripleQuadrupleBeat note
Simple22/4 · 2/2 · 2/83/4 · 3/2 · 3/84/4 · 4/2 · 4/8The lower number names it: 4 = quarter

Reading a compound signature

In compound meter the top number counts divisions, not beats. 6/8 is not six beats — it is two dotted-quarter beats, each dividing into three eighths. Divide the top number by 3 to get the real beat count.

Notating rhythm clearly

When rhythms are notated, it is customary to use rests, beams, ties, and dots so that the metric accents and the individual beats are emphasised rather than obscured. If a reader has to decode where beat 3 falls, the notation has failed.

Syncopations — rhythmic figures that stress normally weak beats or divisions — are frequently notated that way deliberately, with ties and rests that make the displacement visible rather than hiding it inside a longer symbol.

IVTriads and seventh chords

Stack thirds. Everything in tonal harmony is stacked thirds, and the whole vocabulary of chord types falls out of how you stack them.

Triads

Naming the parts

The bottom note of the stack is the root; the others are the third and the fifth, named for their interval above the root. These names stay attached to the notes no matter how the chord is rearranged.

Stack four notes (1–3–5–7) on each degree of any major scale and the qualities are always: Imaj7 · ii7 · iii7 · IVmaj7 · V7 · vi7 · viiø7. Quality reference

  • maj7 — major triad + major 7th. Bright, jazzy, at rest.
  • m7 — minor triad + minor 7th. Smooth, mellow.
  • 7 — major triad + minor 7th. Bluesy, strong tendency to resolve.
  • ø7 / m7♭5 — diminished triad + minor 7th. Tense, unstable.
  • °7 — diminished triad + diminished 7th. Maximum instability; symmetrical.

Why V7 resolves

A dominant seventh contains a tritone between 7̂ (the leading tone) and 4̂. The leading tone wants to rise a half step to 1̂; 4̂ wants to fall a half step to 3̂. Both resolve into the tonic triad at once. That single tritone is the engine of tonal harmony's forward motion.

Inversions and figured bass

The inversion of a triad or seventh chord is determined only by which chord member is in the bass. Any chord member may appear in the soprano or in the other voices without changing the inversion.

Where the numbers come from

Figured-bass numerals are intervals above the bass note, reduced to their simplest useful form. A first-inversion triad (E G C) has a 3rd and a 6th above the bass — the 3rd is assumed, so only the 6 is written. A second-inversion triad (G C E) has a 4th and a 6th, neither assumed, so both are written: 6/4. Learn the derivation once and you never have to memorise the table.

A triad has two inversions (three bass positions). A seventh chord works the same way except that three inversions — four bass positions — are possible.

VHarmony in motion

The bridge from "here are the chords" to "here is why they go in that order." This part is deliberately brief — it is the seam where most of the later material will attach.

Harmonic function

Chords in a key sort into three functional families. Most tonal phrases are a trip through them in one direction, with the return to tonic as the point of arrival.

Tonic

I · vi · iii

Repose. The place the music is heard as leaving from and returning to. vi and iii share notes with I and can stand in for it.

Predominant

IV · ii · ii7

Departure. Prepares the dominant — it contains 4̂ or 6̂ and moves naturally into V.

Dominant

V · V7 · vii°

Tension. Contains the leading tone, and in V7 the tritone. Wants tonic, strongly.

The default direction

T → PD → D → T. Moving forward along that chain sounds normal; skipping ahead sounds normal; moving backward (V → IV) sounds like a deliberate effect, which is exactly why blues and rock use it so much.

Voice-leading basics

Tonal harmony is written as independent melodic lines that happen to make chords — not as a stack of chords that happen to have a top note. Four rules of thumb cover most of it:

  1. Move as little as possible. Keep common tones in the same voice; move the others by step where you can.
  2. Resolve tendency tones. The leading tone (7̂) rises to 1̂. The seventh of a seventh chord falls by step. These are near-absolute.
  3. No parallel fifths or octaves. Two voices a P5 or P8 apart moving to another P5 or P8 collapses their independence — the ear hears one voice, not two.
  4. Watch the outer voices. Soprano and bass are what listeners actually track. Contrary motion between them is the safest default.
Standard spacing

In four-part writing, keep adjacent upper voices (soprano–alto, alto–tenor) within an octave of each other. The bass may sit further away — wide spacing at the bottom of a chord sounds normal, because that is how the overtone series is arranged.
