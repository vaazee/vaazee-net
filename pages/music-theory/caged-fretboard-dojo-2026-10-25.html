Most guitarists learn the neck as a pile of unrelated boxes: a pentatonic shape here, a barre chord there, some licks that only work at the fifth fret. It works until the song changes key. This page builds the opposite: one connected map. It starts with the twelve notes, then the odd tuning that makes the guitar what it is, then octaves and intervals as shapes, then triads. Only then does it reach CAGED, the five chord forms that tile the whole neck in any key. Learn it in order. Every chapter rests on the one before.

一
Chapter One

The Musical Alphabet

Western music uses twelve notes, and then they repeat. Seven have letter names, A B C D E F G, and five sit between them as sharps or flats. One fret on the guitar is one semitone (a half step). Two frets is a whole step.

The one fact to burn in: there is no note between B and C, or between E and F. Every other pair of neighbouring letters has a sharp/flat in between.

Rule of the bladeTwelve frets up = the same note one octave higher. Fret 12 is the open string again, which is why the double dot sits there. Everything from fret 12 to 24 repeats frets 0 to 12.
二
Chapter Two

The Strings & the Crooked Step

Standard tuning, low to high, is E A D G B E (try "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie"). Each string is tuned five frets (a perfect fourth) above the one below it. The fifth fret of the low E is A, which is why you tune the A string to it.

Except once. G to B is only four frets, a major third. This one crooked step is why chord and scale shapes change when they cross onto the B string. Every time a shape passes from the G string to the B string, shift it up one fret to make up for the missing semitone. Chapters 3 and 4 show this again and again.

Learn two strings first

The low E and A strings hold the roots of almost every movable chord and scale shape. Know their natural notes cold and you can find any root in a second or two.

Chapter Four

Intervals as Shapes

An interval is the distance between two notes. Piano players count white and black keys. Guitarists can do better: because the strings are evenly spaced (apart from the B), every interval is a fixed shape from wherever the root is. Move the root and the whole grid moves with it.

The major scale is a fixed set of intervals: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7, built from the step pattern W W H W W W H. Every scale, chord and arpeggio in the rest of this page is a subset or variation of that one recipe.

Triads: Three Notes, One Chord

Stack two thirds and you get a triad, the basic unit of harmony. Every chord shape you know, however many strings it uses, is these three notes repeated in different octaves. An open E major has six strings and only three notes: E, G♯, B.

六
Chapter Six

The CAGED System

Open position gives you five major chords with distinct shapes: C, A, G, E and D. CAGED rests on two observations:

  1. Each shape is movable. Replace the nut with a barre (or just the shape's top strings) and slide it up. The open E shape barred at fret 3 is G major. The open A shape barred at fret 5 is D major. The shape stays the same and only the root changes.
  2. The five shapes lock together in a fixed order. Going up the neck they always run C → A → G → E → D → C… and each one shares its highest root with the next shape's lowest. Five shapes cover all twelve frets, in any key, with no gaps.

Where each shape sits, in five keys

The number is the fret that acts as the shape's "nut", where the barre goes. The order never changes; only the starting point does.

The linking roots C→A share the root on the A string. A→G share the root on the G string. G→E share the root on the low E. E→D share the root on the D string. D→C share the root on the B string. Those roots are the octave shapes from Chapter Three.

Minor CAGED

Every shape has a minor twin: Cm, Am, Gm, Em, Dm. Find the 3rd in each major shape and lower it one fret. The roots and the order stay the same. Some minor forms (Cm, Gm) are awkward to grip as full chords, but they work well as partial voicings and as maps for arpeggios.

One region, three chordsYou rarely need to jump around the neck. In the frets 2–5 area you can play C (A shape, barre 3), F (D shape, root on D3) and G (E shape, barre 3), a whole I–IV–V without moving your hand. CAGED's real use is finding the nearest voicing, not playing five versions of one chord.

Chapter Eight

Scales & Modes on the Shapes

Every CAGED chord shape is the frame for one of the five major-scale positions. Play the chord, then fill in the scale notes around it: the 2 next to the root, the 4 next to the 3, the 6 and 7 around the 5. The chord tones are your landmarks, and the other notes are steps between them. That's why soloing "inside the shape" sounds musical: you always know which notes are the chord.

Pentatonic boxes

Remove the 4th and 7th from the major scale and you get the major pentatonic (1 2 3 5 6). Those five notes, read from the 6th degree, are the relative minor pentatonic (1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7). The famous "box 1" A-minor pentatonic at fret 5 is the same set of notes as C major pentatonic in its G shape. The five pentatonic boxes are the five CAGED shapes with notes removed. Use the Dojo's Rel. minor pent layer to see it.

Modes: same notes, new home

A mode is the major scale played from a different degree, so the same note becomes home. On the neck you don't need new patterns. You keep the parent scale's five positions and move the root. D Dorian uses the notes of C major, so it uses C major's five shapes, with the D notes as home base.

CAGED vs. three-notes-per-stringCAGED positions have two or three notes per string and line up with chord shapes, which makes them good for chord-tone soloing and rhythm playing. Three-notes-per-string (3NPS) systems give seven even patterns built for speed and legato. They don't compete. Learn CAGED for knowing where you are, and add 3NPS later if you want speed.
九
Chapter Nine

The Practice Regimen

Knowing the map isn't the same as being able to use it. These drills take 20–30 minutes a day. Do the first few every day until they feel easy, then rotate the rest in.

5 min

Name the note

Pick random frets on the low E and A strings and name each note out loud. Then go the other way: given a note, find it on both strings. Aim for under two seconds per answer.

5 min

Octave hunt

Choose one note. Using only octave shapes, find every copy of it from fret 0 to 15, low string to high, then back down. Change the note each day.

5 min

One chord, five places

Pick a chord (start with D). Play it in all five CAGED shapes going up the neck, then coming down. Say each shape's name as you play it.

5 min

Arpeggio links

Play the triad as single notes through one shape, low to high, then slide into the next shape on the shared root. Continue across all five without stopping.

5 min

Interval singing

Play a root, sing the 3rd, then check it on the neck. Do the same for the 5th, ♭7 and 6. You're tying the shapes to your ear, which is where they matter.

5 min

Stay in one region

Choose four frets (for example 5–8) and play a I–IV–V–vi progression without leaving them. You'll need to choose a different CAGED shape for each chord.

5 min

Single-string melody

Play a simple tune (a nursery rhyme, a riff) along one string. This builds the horizontal sense that boxes leave out.

10 min

Land on chord tones

Over a backing track or drone, improvise in one scale position but end every phrase on a root, 3rd or 5th. Then do it in the next position.

Gotchas on the path

  • The B-string shift catches everyone. If a shape sounds wrong only on the top two strings, you forgot the +1 fret.
  • CAGED is a map, not a method. Nobody solos by thinking "now I'm in the G shape". You practise the map until it fades into the background and you just know where the notes are.
  • Don't get stuck in boxes. Shapes are where you start. Slide between them, play along single strings, and think in chord tones, not finger patterns.
  • Learn intervals, not dots. Set the Dojo labels to intervals. "That's the ♭7" carries over to every key; "that's the third dot" doesn't.
  • Full shapes are rare in real music. Most real voicings are fragments, three or four strings of a CAGED shape. The full form is there to teach you where those fragments come from.
  • Learn one key properly first. Get C or G down across the whole neck before touching E♭. The geometry doesn't change between keys; only where it starts does.

First the shapes. Then the notes. Then neither, only the music.
