The formula: five notes out of seven

A pentatonic scale is a seven-note scale with its two tension notes removed. Major pentatonic takes the major scale and drops the 4th and 7th, which leaves 1 2 3 5 6. Minor pentatonic takes the natural minor scale and drops the 2nd and ♭6, which leaves 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. With no half-steps left, nearly every note sounds good over the chord. That's why it's usually the first scale guitarists improvise with.

What changes: both scales keep the 1 and the 5 (C and G, the power-chord frame). The other three notes change: 2→4, 3→♭3, 6→♭7. The two step patterns are rotations of each other. Both point to the main idea of this page: the five notes of one pentatonic can be started from any of them.

To move these boxes to another key, shift every fret by the same amount. For Em, subtract 5: the boxes start at frets 0, 3, 5, 7 and 10. For Fm, subtract 4: they start at frets 1, 4, 6, 8 and 11. Each box shares its top notes with the next box's bottom notes, and after box 5 the cycle starts again at box 1, 12 frets higher.

Major pentatonic uses the same shapes

Every minor pentatonic box is also a major pentatonic box. Only the note you treat as home changes. C major pentatonic and A minor pentatonic have exactly the same five notes (C D E G A). The minor version starts on A, the 6th of C.

The 3-fret rule

To play a major pentatonic, use the minor pentatonic shape 3 frets lower. A minor box 1 is at fret 5, so A major box 1 is the same shape at fret 2, because it shares its notes with F♯ minor.

Change the home note

The Am box at fret 5 is also a C major pentatonic box. If you resolve phrases to the C on the low E string (fret 8) instead of the A at fret 5, the same shape sounds major.

Blues notes, bends and a lick

Minor blues = minor pent + ♭5

A C D E♭ E G. In box 1, the E♭ is at A string fret 6 and G string fret 8. Slide through it rather than landing on it.

Major blues = major pent + ♭3

C D E♭ E G A. Bending or hammering from the ♭3 to the 3 is the classic country and blues sound.

Connecting the boxes

  • Slide between boxes on one string, e.g. G string 7 → 9 to move from box 1 into box 2.
  • Two-notes-per-string diagonal runs move up the neck as you go across the strings.
  • One-string practice: play the whole scale on a single string, from fret 0 to 17. This shows the 3-2-2-3-2 spacing directly.
  • Extended "Albert King" box: box 1 plus the top part of box 2 (frets 8–10 on the B and high e strings). A lot of blues licks live here.

Pentatonic substitution

Over Dm7

D minor pentatonic gives the basic sound. A minor adds the 9th. E minor gives a Dorian color.

Over Cmaj7

A minor or E minor pentatonic sounds smooth. B minor adds the ♯4 for a Lydian sound.

Practice ideas & songs to learn from

Practice

  1. Each box in sequence: play in groups of 3 or 4 notes (1-2-3, 2-3-4…), ascending and descending, with a metronome.
  2. Target chord tones: over a backing track, end every phrase on a note of the current chord.
  3. Call and response: play a 2-bar phrase, then answer it with a variation.
  4. Limit yourself: improvise using only 3 notes, or only 2 strings. This builds phrasing instead of running up and down the scale.
  5. Change keys: play the same lick in Em (fret 0/12), Fm (fret 1) and Am (fret 5).

Gotchas

  • Major to minor isn't "flatten two notes." Going from C major pentatonic to C minor pentatonic changes three notes: 2→4, 3→♭3, 6→♭7. Only Sa and Pa (1 and 5) stay.
  • The blue note is a passing tone. The ♭5 sounds tense if you hold it. Slide, bend or hammer through it.
  • Bends must land on a scale note. A bend that stops short of its target sounds out of tune, not bluesy, unless you mean the quarter-step on the ♭3.
  • The boxes don't tell you where home is. The same shape is Am, C major, Mohanam or Hindolam depending on which note you resolve to. Know your root positions.
  • A scale isn't a raga. The Carnatic matches here are scale equivalents. The gamakas and phrasing are what make a raga.
