In brief · tear here

Most of the time, people say yes on autopilot. With too much information to process, we lean on mental shortcuts, and those shortcuts usually work well. Each one rests on a social rule that holds a society together: repay favours, stay consistent, follow the crowd, agree with people you like, defer to experts, grab what is scarce, and (added in 2021) favour your own group.

Cialdini calls salespeople, fundraisers, recruiters, advertisers and con artists compliance professionals. They have learned to fake the triggers for those rules. His evidence comes from his own experiments and from three years working undercover in sales trainings. The book teaches you to spot each weapon of influence and to refuse it when the trigger is fake. No book about why we comply is easier to read, and few are used more widely.

"I can admit it freely now. All my life I've been a patsy."— the opening line of Influence

The historical moment

Early-1980s America was full of selling. Direct mail and telemarketing were booming, and so were Tupperware parties, Amway networks, haggling on the car lot, and televangelists asking viewers to "send in your seed money." Inside psychology, the field had just finished its great age of situationist experiments: Milgram's obedience studies, Asch's conformity lines, Festinger's cognitive dissonance, Darley and Latané's bystanders, Zimbardo's prison. It was also starting to absorb Kahneman and Tversky's work on heuristics (1974). Cialdini connected the two worlds. He took what the lab had learned and showed how the sales floor was already using it.

The occasion

Cialdini says he kept buying things he didn't want, from magazine subscriptions to sanitation-ball tickets. He wanted to know why, and he decided that lab studies alone would not tell him. So he became a participant observer. He answered newspaper ads for sales trainees and went undercover in encyclopedia and vacuum-cleaner sales, portrait photography, used-car lots, fundraising, telemarketing, advertising and PR.

The scope

The book attempts a complete taxonomy of compliance. Thousands of tactics, Cialdini argues, come down to a handful of psychological principles. Learn the principles and you will recognise the tactics, whether you meet them on a car lot, in a cult recruitment pitch or on a booking website.

02The Author

A field psychologist, a self-confessed patsy, and later an Arizona professor and international speaker.

Who he is. Robert Beno Cialdini grew up in an Italian-American family in Milwaukee. He has said that the household's tangle of obligation, kinship and neighbourhood loyalty taught him about reciprocity and, much later, about what he would call unity. He studied at the University of Wisconsin, took his PhD in social psychology at the University of North Carolina, did postgraduate work at Columbia, and spent nearly his whole career at Arizona State. He became the most cited researcher on compliance in social psychology, founded the consultancy Influence at Work, and became a fixture on the corporate speaking circuit.

Field over lab

His best-known experiments happen in ordinary places: towel-reuse cards in hotel bathrooms, theft signs in the Petrified Forest, students wearing university sweatshirts the Monday after a football win ("basking in reflected glory").

Norms

His central subject is norms, the unwritten rules a society runs on, and how each one can be turned against the people who follow it. Later he separated descriptive norms (what people do) from injunctive norms (what people approve of).

The patsy's voice

His style is dry and self-deprecating, and he often casts himself as the one being fooled. His most memorable defeat comes at the hands of a Boy Scout selling chocolate bars he did not want.

Why the biography matters. The undercover years are what make the book work. Without them, Influence would be a good review of the research. With them, it reads partly as a confession and partly as a spy's report.

ReputationHe is treated as a founder of applied behavioural science and is read in business schools, marketing teams, political campaigns and government "nudge units". Charlie Munger praised the book in his 1995 Harvard talk on human misjudgment and, by Cialdini's account, sent him a share of Berkshire Hathaway as thanks. The usual worry is that the book doubles as a manual for manipulators. Cialdini answers that the defences are the whole point of the book.

How it ranks. Pre-Suasion may be the deeper book, a subtle study of attention and framing, but nobody calls it Cialdini's masterpiece. Most of what the public knows of him comes from Influence, which turned "the six principles" into everyday business vocabulary.

In its genre. The book sits in a long line of exposés of persuasion: Bernays's Propaganda (1928), Packard's The Hidden Persuaders (1957), Hoffer's The True Believer (1951). It also anticipates behavioural economics: Ariely, Thaler and Sunstein, and Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow. Packard warned readers about persuaders. Cialdini explains how they work. Kahneman explains why the mind is built so they can. Cialdini's book is the one you can put to use most directly.

04The Argument

One mechanism, seven principles, and a defence for each.

The central thesis: click, whirr

The book opens with a finding from the ethologist M. W. Fox. A mother turkey cares for any chick that makes a cheep-cheep sound and attacks any chick that doesn't, even her own. Fox fitted a stuffed polecat, the turkey's natural enemy, with a tape recorder playing that sound, and the hen gathered it under her wing. A single feature of the situation, the trigger, sets off a whole fixed program of behaviour.

Cialdini argues that people work the same way. Click: a trigger appears. Whirr: the response runs. These shortcuts are usually adaptive, because nobody has time to think through every decision. But a dependable shortcut can also be exploited. Anyone who can fake the trigger gets the response, even when the conditions that make the response sensible are missing.

The turquoise sale

An Arizona jeweller's turquoise would not sell. She left a note telling staff to price it "× ½"; an employee read it as "× 2". At double the price it sold out, because buyers took a high price to mean high quality.

The magic "because"

In Ellen Langer's photocopier study, a request with no reason worked 60% of the time. "Because I have to make copies", which explains nothing, worked 93% of the time. A real reason ("I'm in a rush") worked 94%.

The contrast principle

The second item is judged against the first. Clothing salesmen sell the suit before the sweater. Estate agents show a run-down "setup" house before the one they want to sell. A student's letter home lists a fire, a hospital stay and a pregnancy, then reveals that none of it happened. The real news is a D in chemistry.

Interactive: the contrast principle

Shown after a $495 suit, the $95 sweater feels like an afterthought. The salesperson sells the expensive item first, so the extras seem small.

1Weapons of Influencethe mechanism

Introduces the fixed-action pattern (the turkey and the polecat), the "because" study, the turquoise sale and the contrast principle. It also introduces jujitsu, Cialdini's image for the professional's method: they use the target's own forces, the principles, instead of applying force themselves. That is why the target rarely notices.

2ReciprocationThe Old Give and Take… and Takereciprocity

"Repay, in kind, what another person has provided us."

The rule appears in every known society. In 1985, famine-hit Ethiopia sent $5,000 in earthquake aid to Mexico to repay help Mexico had given in 1935. It works even when the giver is disliked, even when the gift was never requested, and it can lead to uneven exchanges where a small favour wins a large one. Its cleverest form is rejection-then-retreat: ask for something large, accept the refusal, then make a smaller request. The retreat looks like a concession, and people feel obliged to concede in return.

The studies
  • Regan's Coke study: an unrequested soft drink from "Joe" roughly doubled the raffle tickets subjects later bought from him. How much they liked Joe made little difference.
  • Hare Krishna airport flowers and Disabled American Veterans address labels: including the labels nearly doubled the donation rate, from about 18% to 35%.
  • The Boy Scout: he asked Cialdini to buy $5 circus tickets. Cialdini refused, then bought two $1 chocolate bars, and he doesn't like chocolate.
  • The juvenile-detention chaperone study (Cialdini et al., 1975): asked directly, about 17% agreed to chaperone a zoo trip. After first refusing a two-year counselling commitment, 50% agreed.
  • Watergate: Liddy's $1-million "Gemstone" plan was rejected twice. The $250,000 version, which led to the break-in, was approved.
  • Free samples, the Amway "BUG" (a bag of products left at the home on trial), restaurant mints with the bill.
How to say noAccept real favours and concessions graciously, but if a "gift" turns out to be a sales device, redefine it as one. The rule says favours must be repaid. Tricks don't count.
3Commitment & ConsistencyHobgoblins of the Mindconsistency

Once we have taken a stand, we feel pressure, from ourselves and others, to behave consistently with it.

Consistency usually works in our favour: it signals integrity and saves us from rethinking every decision. That is why it can be exploited. The most effective commitments are active (written down), public, effortful and owned, meaning the person feels they chose freely. They work by changing how a person sees themselves, and the new self-image then produces its own reasons for the commitment.

The studies
  • Racetrack bettors are more confident in their horse right after placing the bet.
  • Freedman & Fraser (1966), the foot-in-the-door: people who had agreed to display a three-inch "Be a Safe Driver" sign later accepted a huge "DRIVE CAREFULLY" billboard on their lawn at about 76%, against about 17% for people asked cold.
  • Chinese indoctrination of American POWs in Korea: prisoners were led from small written admissions ("America is not perfect") to essays read on the camp radio, with token prizes for participation. The commitments were active and public, and the rewards were small enough that prisoners felt they had made the choice themselves.
  • Hazing: fraternity Hell Week and the Thonga initiation rite. Aronson and Mills found that a harder initiation makes people value the group more.
  • Written goals at Amway; toy-company Christmas undersupply (parents who promised a toy buy it in January).
  • The low-ball: the car dealer offers a great price, the buyer commits, then the price advantage disappears, and the buyer usually goes ahead anyway.
How to say noPay attention to stomach signs, the knot that tells you you're being pushed into a consistency you don't want. Then check your heart of hearts: "Knowing what I know now, if I could go back, would I make the same choice?"
4Social ProofTruths Are Usconsensus

We decide what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct.

Social proof is strongest under two conditions: uncertainty and similarity. When people don't know what to do, they look at others, especially others like themselves. Canned laughter works even on people who say they hate it. Bartenders "salt" the tip jar. Advertisers call products "fastest-growing". In a crowd, everyone may be watching everyone else look calm. The result is pluralistic ignorance: each person concludes that nothing is wrong because no one else seems worried.

The studies
  • Festinger, When Prophecy Fails: when the flying saucer failed to arrive, the cult members who were together began recruiting. Without physical proof, they needed more believers to keep their own belief going.
  • Kitty Genovese (1964) and Darley & Latané's bystander experiments. The famous "38 witnesses" account is now known to be badly exaggerated (see §10).
  • David Phillips and the Werther effect: suicides, car crashes and plane crashes rose after front-page suicide stories, especially among people similar to the person in the story.
  • Bandura: children afraid of dogs lost much of their fear after watching films of other children playing with dogs.
  • Jonestown (1978): more than 900 people, isolated in a jungle and unsure what to do, followed each other's lead.
How to say noWatch for fake evidence: staged testimonials, canned applause, invented "bestseller" claims. Remember that a crowd may simply be copying itself. And in an emergency, single out one person: "You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance."
5LikingThe Friendly Thiefrapport

We prefer to say yes to people we know and like.

At a Tupperware party the real salesperson is the hostess, who is your friend. Joe Girard, the Guinness-listed "world's greatest car salesman", sent every customer a card each month with one message: I like you. Cialdini lists the factors that produce liking: physical attractiveness, which creates a halo effect; similarity; compliments; contact and cooperation; and conditioning, or association.

The studies
  • Attractiveness: attractive candidates won more than two and a half times as many votes in one Canadian federal election; attractive defendants receive lighter sentences.
  • Similarity: people are more likely to give a dime to someone dressed like them.
  • Cooperation: at the Robbers Cave camp, Sherif reconciled rival groups of boys by giving them shared goals. Aronson's jigsaw classroom used the same idea. Good cop/bad cop is cooperation used as a manipulation.
  • Association: Persian messengers were rewarded or killed according to their news, and weathermen still get hate mail when it rains. Sports fans say "we won" but "they lost" (Cialdini's BIRGing studies). Razran's "luncheon technique" showed that people like ideas more when they hear them over food.
How to say noYou don't need to spot every source of liking. Notice when you like a salesperson more than you should, given how briefly you've known them, then judge the deal separately from the person.
6AuthorityDirected Deferenceobedience

We feel a strong pressure to obey legitimate authority.

The chapter opens with Milgram: about 65% of ordinary participants went on to the maximum 450-volt shock because a man in a lab coat told them to continue. Real authority is a useful shortcut. Its outward signs are easy to fake, and Cialdini names three: titles, clothes and trappings.

The studies
  • "Rear" ear: a prescription for "R ear" (right ear) was misread, and the nurse put the ear drops in the patient's rectum without questioning it.
  • Hofling's hospital study: 21 of 22 nurses were about to give a dangerous dose of an unauthorised drug ("Astroten") on a phone order from a "doctor" they had never met.
  • Captainitis: co-pilots stay silent while the captain makes a fatal mistake.
  • Titles make people seem taller. Uniforms: in Bickman's studies, people followed orders from a man dressed as a security guard. Trappings: drivers wait longer before honking at a luxury car.
  • Robert Young, TV's "Marcus Welby, M.D.", advertised Sanka coffee playing the doctor he only portrayed. Vincent the waiter steered diners away from an expensive dish to look trustworthy, then sold them the wine.
How to say noAsk two questions. "Is this authority truly an expert?" (in the relevant field, not just in costume) and "How truthful can we expect this expert to be here?" Be careful when someone admits a small weakness, because the admission may be setting up a larger claim.
7ScarcityThe Rule of the Fewreactance

Opportunities seem more valuable to us when they are less available.

There are two reasons. Scarce things are usually better, which makes scarcity a reasonable shortcut. And losing access to something threatens our freedom, which produces psychological reactance (Brehm): we want it more. Typical tactics are "limited number", "deadline" and staged competition for the "last one". Information works the same way: censored information becomes more desirable and more believable.

The studies
  • The Romeo & Juliet effect: couples whose parents interfered loved each other more.
  • Dade County detergent ban: after Miami banned phosphate detergents, residents smuggled and hoarded them and rated them as cleaning better.
  • The beef importer (Knishinsky): mentioning a coming shortage roughly doubled orders. Adding that the information was "exclusive" raised orders about sixfold.
  • Worchel's cookie jar: cookies from a jar holding 2 were rated better than the same cookies from a jar holding 10. They were rated best when the jar went from 10 to 2, and best of all when the cookies had gone to other people. Newly scarce and contested beats always scarce.
  • Davies' J-curve of revolutions; the 1985 New Coke backlash.
How to say noTreat the rush of excitement as your signal. Calm down, then ask why you want the item. If you want it to use, remember that a scarce cookie tastes no better than any other.
EEpilogue · Instant InfluencePrimitive Consent for an Automatic Agethe stakes

Modern life is faster and more crowded with information, so we depend on shortcuts more, not less. That makes faking them a kind of pollution of a resource everyone shares. Cialdini calls for more than personal caution. He urges retaliation: boycotting, reporting and refusing the people who fake the triggers, so that the shortcuts stay trustworthy.

8UnityThe We Is the Shared Me · 2021identity

We say yes to people we see as part of "us".

The 2021 edition's seventh principle. Unity is about identity, not similarity. It goes deeper than liking: a shared identity makes the other person's interests feel like our own. Cialdini names two sources: being together (kinship, real or implied, as in "brother"; shared place, such as home, neighbourhood or region) and acting together (singing, marching or dancing in step; shared hardship; co-creation: asking for someone's advice rather than their opinion makes them a partner).

How to say noBe suspicious of claimed kinship ("as a fellow parent…", "hey, brother") and of anyone who tries to define an "us" and a "them" for you. The 2021 edition offers fewer defences here than for the other principles, perhaps because identity is the hardest thing to set aside.

Two ways up the staircase

Two of the book's best-known techniques move in opposite directions. The foot-in-the-door (consistency) starts small and builds. The door-in-the-face (reciprocity) starts with an extreme request and retreats. Switch between them:

Foot-in-the-door (Freedman & Fraser, 1966): a tiny 'Be a Safe Driver' sticker comes first. Having said yes, people see themselves as civic-minded, and about 76% later accept an ugly billboard on the lawn (vs ~17% asked cold). The lever is consistency with a new self-image.

1984 vs 2021: how the principles were reordered

The 2021 edition is more than a reprint. Commitment moved from the second chapter to the sixth, and a seventh principle, Unity, appeared at the end. The new order roughly runs from building a relationship (give, like, show consensus, show credentials) to the deeper levers (scarcity, commitment, shared identity).

Also new in 2021

  • Reader's Reports: readers' own encounters with each principle.
  • Much more about online persuasion: reviews, ratings and social feeds, all forms of social proof.
  • A sustained discussion of ethics, plus long endnotes that respond to some challenges to the older studies.

Which edition to read?

For speed, the 2006/07 Collins classic: about 320 tight pages. For the complete current version, the 2021 New and Expanded, which adds Unity, online examples and the ethics discussion. Its extra length comes mostly from new examples rather than new ideas.

The conclusionMental shortcuts are part of being human and usually serve us well. Persuasion becomes exploitation when the trigger is faked. Each principle has an honest use (a real gift, real consensus, real expertise) and a counterfeit one. The reader's job is to tell the two apart, and the persuader's job is to stay on the honest side.

05Key Figures & Concepts

The people, studies and ideas the book keeps returning to.

The narrator-patsy

Cialdini himself: half scientist, half reformed mark. Opening with his own gullibility makes the reader trust him, which, as the book later shows, is itself an influence technique.

The compliance professional

A recurring type: salesperson, fundraiser, recruiter, con artist, cult leader. Cialdini doesn't treat them as villains. They are practitioners who learned by trial and error what psychologists learned by experiment, and they are good at it, which is what makes them unsettling.

The turkey & the polecat

The book's founding image (from M. W. Fox): a single sound sets off a whole fixed behaviour, even when the sound comes from a predator.

Langer · Milgram · Festinger

Three borrowed classics: the "because" study (mindlessness), the obedience experiments (authority), and When Prophecy Fails (dissonance and social proof).

Freedman & Fraser · Regan · Brehm

The foot-in-the-door (consistency), the Coke-and-raffle study (reciprocity), and psychological reactance (scarcity).

Joe Girard · Liddy · Jim Jones

A car salesman who mailed "I like you", a Watergate plan approved after two rejections, and a jungle colony that followed itself into mass death.

06Themes & Key Takeaways

What the book is about, and what you can use from it.

Major themes

1 · Automaticity is efficient

Shortcuts are reasonable tools for a mind with limited attention. The problem is counterfeits, not shortcuts.

2 · Social rules can be turned against us

Reciprocity, consistency and deference are good social rules, and that is exactly why they make good weapons.

3 · Two routes to the same knowledge

Salespeople and experimenters arrived at the same principles independently. That agreement is evidence the principles are real.

4 · The self is the lever

Commitment works by changing how people see themselves. Unity works by enlarging who counts as "us".

5 · Uncertainty plus similarity produces conformity

Social proof is strongest when people don't know what to do and when others like them are deciding.

6 · Defence needs anger, too

Faking a trigger damages a resource everyone shares. Cialdini thinks it should be punished, not just avoided.

Key takeaways

  1. When you notice yourself about to say yes, ask which principle is working on you and whether the trigger is real.
  2. Accept favours, but not as tactics. If a "gift" turns out to be a sales device, you owe nothing.
  3. Small commitments lead to big ones. Watch for the small "yes" designed to change how you see yourself, and ask the heart-of-hearts question.
  4. In an emergency, choose one person and give them one specific task.
  5. Liking the salesperson is not a reason to like the car. Judge the deal separately from the person.
  6. Ask any authority two questions: are they an expert in this, and are they being honest here?
  7. When you feel the rush of scarcity, slow down. Scarcity changes how much you want something, not how useful it is.
  8. To persuade ethically, point to principles that are already there (real consensus, real expertise, real scarcity) instead of inventing them.
  9. Ask for advice, not opinions. Co-creation builds unity.
  10. Order matters. A big request first makes a small one look reasonable, and an expensive option first makes the next one look cheap.

Motifs & the title

Motifs

  • Machinery: click, whirr, tape recorders, programs. People described as mechanisms.
  • Weapons: the arsenal metaphor casts the reader as someone under attack.
  • The patsy: the author as the victim who learned from it.
  • Jujitsu: the target's own strength is used against them, so they rarely notice.

The title

"Influence" is deliberately neutral: not "Manipulation", not quite "Persuasion". It covers both honest and counterfeit uses. The original subtitle, How and Why People Agree to Things, is actually more precise. The book is about the "yes", not about arguments.

07Style & Craft

Plain prose, a wry tone, and the same structure in every chapter.

Prose. Plain, conversational American English. Paragraphs are short and the reader is addressed directly ("Have you ever…?"). The jargon is light and explained, and the humour is understated.

The structure each chapter repeats

Narrative technique

A first-person confession combined with case studies. Framing the research as "going undercover" gives the book the feel of a spy story, and the reader is cast as both the target and the investigator.

Voice

Self-deprecating, curious, and a little indignant. Cialdini can be very funny: the letter from the daughter, Vincent the waiter, the author outmanoeuvred by a twelve-year-old Boy Scout.

The notable passage

Chapter 1 · page 1

"I can admit it freely now. All my life I've been a patsy." In one line, a science book becomes a confession, and the author stands beside the reader rather than above them. A book about influence opens by earning trust through an admission, the same "candid admission" tactic the authority chapter later warns about. Whether or not he meant it, the first line demonstrates what the book describes.

"Click, whirr" is one of the best phrases in popular science: two words that carry the whole thesis.

08Philosophical & Intellectual Analysis

Bounded rationality, the ethics of persuasion, and trust as public infrastructure.

Worldview. Cialdini combines a bounded-rationality view of the mind (Herbert Simon, Kahneman and Tversky) with an evolutionary-functional view of social norms. People are social animals whose cooperation depends on rules applied quickly. His moral position resembles thinking about a commons: shortcuts are a public good, and people who fake them are free riders polluting a shared supply of trust.

Tensions & contradictions

A handbook that cuts both ways

The book promises self-defence, but its examples work just as well as instructions. Its largest audience is in sales and marketing, and Cialdini welcomed that audience. Can one book be both the vaccine and the virus?

Where honest ends

Is a "limited-time" offer honest if stock really is limited? Is a sincere gift that also creates obligation a tactic? The ethical line depends on intent and accuracy, which are often hard to see from outside.

The reasonable shortcut

If shortcuts are reasonable, then falling for them is reasonable too. Yet the book asks us to override them, which means paying the cost of deliberation it says we can't afford.

Unity and tribalism

The 2021 chapter presents in-group feeling both as a persuasion tool and as the cause of polarisation, without fully deciding whether readers should use it or resist it.

Interactive: honest vs counterfeit

Cialdini's ethical test is that persuasion is legitimate when the principle is actually present, and exploitative when it is manufactured. Click a coupon to flip it from the honest use to the counterfeit one.

The deeper claimTrust is infrastructure. A society runs on signals no one can check personally: titles, crowds, favours, promises. Every faked signal makes that signal less reliable for everyone. Influence is partly a book about protecting a shared resource of trust, so its moral is civic as well as personal.

The ethical question. Is it wrong to persuade people by means they don't consciously weigh? Cialdini's answer is no, provided the principle is real. Pointing to genuine consensus helps someone decide. Inventing consensus takes the decision away from them. That position matches the distinction philosophers draw between persuasion, which respects a person's agency, and manipulation, which bypasses it.

10Criticism & Counterpoints

Where the book has aged, and how well its evidence has held up.

Case study: the 38 witnesses

As the book tells it (from the 1964 New York Times): 38 respectable neighbours watched Kitty Genovese being attacked over 35 minutes and none of them called the police. Cialdini uses the case to explain pluralistic ignorance: each witness took the others' inaction as a sign that nothing was wrong.

Common critiques

"A manual for manipulators"

The most frequent objection: the defence sections are much shorter than the descriptions of tactics. Response: understanding the mechanism is the defence, and the later editions put far more weight on ethics.

Anecdote and confirmation

The examples are chosen to illustrate, not to test, and almost any persuasive episode can be fitted to some principle after the fact. Is the taxonomy predictive, or a set of labels applied afterwards?

Dated material

Tupperware parties, encyclopedia salesmen, mail-order clubs, 1980s television advertising. The 2021 edition updates much of it, but the classic edition reads like a time capsule.

Culture and WEIRD samples

The principles are presented as universal, but their strength varies between cultures. Consistency pressure is weaker in collectivist cultures, where social proof tends to be stronger.

Compliance, not conviction

The book covers getting people to say yes, not changing their minds through argument. Petty and Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model covers both the reasoned route and the shortcut route. Cialdini covers only the shortcut route.

Small classic studies

Many of the famous experiments predate the replication crisis and had small samples. Pairing them with vivid anecdotes can make the effects seem more certain than they are.

The case against, and the defenceAgainst: seven well-known principles, a stack of memorable but sometimes unreliable experiments, and examples that can't be falsified, in a book that has taught more people to persuade than to resist. For: no other book gives ordinary readers such a usable vocabulary for the pressures they meet every day. Its lists have lasted because readers keep recognising them in their own lives. Some of the details are wrong, but the overall framework still holds.

11Who Should Read It & How

Almost everyone who buys or sells anything, which is almost everyone.

Ideal reader

Anyone who buys, sells, negotiates, manages, raises money, runs campaigns or designs products, and anyone tired of being a "patsy". It is especially valuable for people in sales, UX and policy.

Prerequisites

None. The book is self-contained and accessible to any reader.

How to read it

Read one chapter at a time, with a notebook. After each chapter, spend a day noticing that principle in shops, emails, apps and meetings. Read each "How to Say No" section twice. Give the broad principles more weight than any single statistic (see §10).

If you read nothing else

Chapter 1 + Commitment & Consistency

Chapter 1, "Weapons of Influence", gives you the "click, whirr" framework that organises the rest of the book.

Commitment and Consistency is the deepest material in the book: the POW camps, the low-ball, the Christmas toy trick, and the heart-of-hearts question you can use for the rest of your life.
