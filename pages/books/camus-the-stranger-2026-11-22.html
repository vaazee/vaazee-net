Télégramme · Asile de Marengo → AlgerIn brief

Mère décédée. Enterrement demain. Sentiments distingués. — the telegram that opens the book

A French clerk in colonial Algiers buries his mother without crying, takes up with a woman, does a favour for a shady neighbour, and on a blinding afternoon on the beach shoots an Arab man who is never named. The second half is his trial, where the court spends far less time on the killing than on his failure to weep.

Camus's first novel turns flatness into a question: what happens to a man who refuses to say more than he feels? It is the founding fiction of the absurd. Since the 1950s it has also been read as a colonial novel with a hole in the middle where the victim should be. Listen for the gap between the sun (what Meursault feels) and the story (what society needs him to have felt).

"Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don't know."— Part I, Ch. 1 · tr. Matthew Ward (1988)

Nº 01Identity & Context

A tiny book, written in Algiers, finished in Paris weeks before the Germans arrived, and published under occupation.

The making of the book

  • 1936–38La Mort heureuse: an abandoned first novel. Its hero, Patrice Mersault, kills a man for money to buy happiness. Camus keeps the name, the sun and the sea, and throws away the plot.
  • 1938–39Reporter for Alger Républicain, covering murder trials and the hunger in Kabylie ("Misère de la Kabylie").
  • 1939A real beach fight involving friends of Camus, near Oran, becomes the seed of Chapter 6.
  • May 1940Manuscript finished in Paris, weeks before the German army reached the city.
  • June 1942Published by Gallimard in occupied Paris after the censor passed it as apolitical.
  • Oct 1942Le Mythe de Sisyphe, the essay twin, appears.
  • Feb 1943Sartre's "Explication de L'Étranger" in Cahiers du Sud sets the reading for a generation.
  • 1946Stuart Gilbert's English translation: The Outsider (London), The Stranger (New York, Knopf).

Colonial Algeria: who counts

French Algeria was not a colony in law but three French départements. That legal fiction hid a hierarchy of rights the novel takes for granted. In the book, Europeans have names, jobs, gossip and lawyers. The Arabs are "the Arabs".

European settlers"pieds-noirs" · full French citizens~0.95 M
Arabs & Berbers"indigènes" · mostly without citizenship~6.2 M

1936 census, rounded. In the novel, the larger group gets one unnamed victim, his unnamed sister, and a few men standing silently on a beach.

The "three absurds"Camus planned his work in cycles. The first, the cycle of the absurd, has a novel (L'Étranger), an essay (Le Mythe de Sisyphe) and a play (Caligula). The novel was meant to show what the essay argues: life has no meaning given in advance, and the honest response is neither suicide nor hope but lucid revolt.

Nº 02The Author

A poor boy from Belcourt who became a Nobel laureate at 44 and died in a car crash at 46.

Belcourt

Born 1913 in Mondovi, eastern Algeria. His father died at the Marne when Albert was an infant. His mother, Catherine Sintès, was partly deaf, could not read, and cleaned houses. A teacher, Louis Germain, got him to the lycée, and Camus dedicated his Nobel lecture to him. At 17 he had his first attack of tuberculosis, which ended his goalkeeping and stayed with him for life.

Sensibility

He wrote about the body and the sun before he wrote ideas: sea, heat, a pagan happiness without God (Noces, 1938). Then the absurd, "the confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world". He hated the social lie and pretended feelings, and he opposed the death penalty all his life.

Threads into the novel

Belcourt's cafés and stairwells, an old man with a dog, a warehouse-keeper rumoured to be a pimp. Courtroom reporting taught him how a trial turns a life into a story. A family legend that his father vomited after watching an execution appears in the last chapter almost word for word.

Reputation

He refused the "existentialist" label. His 1952 break with Sartre over communism left him isolated on the Left for decades. His refusal to back the FLN in the Algerian war still divides readers. Today he is often seen as right about totalitarianism and tragically stuck about Algeria.

Nº 03Where It Sits in the Canon

His debut novel, and in nearly everyone's view his masterpiece: the first panel of the "absurd" triptych.

The essay twin

The Myth of Sisyphus came out four months later. It gives the scaffolding the novel deliberately hides: no appeal, no hope, lucid revolt. The novel is the essay's philosophy shown in images.

The answer

The Plague (1947) moves from "I" to "we". Dr Rieux acts with others against an absurd evil, and is Meursault's moral opposite. The Rebel: "I rebel — therefore we exist."

The dark mirror

The Fall (1956): Clamence confesses everything and is sincere about nothing. Meursault confesses nothing and is sincere about everything.

Hidden in the cellThe yellowed newspaper story Meursault rereads in prison, about a man murdered by his own mother and sister who didn't recognise him, became Camus's play Le Malentendu (1944). One absurd story is folded inside another.

Nº 04Plot

Two halves, the second rewriting the first. Part I: eighteen days of sun. Part II: eleven months of words.

The diptych

Part I6 chapters · the sun~18 days
Part II5 chapters · the words~11 months

Same number of pages, roughly twenty times the elapsed time. Prison erases days ("yesterday" and "tomorrow" lose their meaning), and the trial compresses a year into a story.

Chapter by chapter

Part II is hidden until you ask for it. It contains the trial, the verdict and the ending.

  • I.1The funeralMarengo, Thursday–Fridaysun ●●●●

    A telegram from the old people's home. He asks his boss for two days off. He declines to see the body, smokes and drinks café au lait during the vigil, and dozes among Maman's silent friends. The funeral walks under a savage sun. Thomas Pérez, Maman's "fiancé" at the home, limps behind, cuts across fields, and faints at the grave. Blood-red earth, white roots. Back in Algiers, twelve hours of sleep.

  • I.2Saturday, Sundaythe port, the cinema, the balconysea

    A swim at the port. Marie Cardona, a former typist from his office. A Fernandel comedy, and the night together. She flinches for a moment when she hears about Maman. Sunday he spends watching the street from his balcony, from noon to dark. Nothing has changed.

  • I.3NeighboursSalamano, Raymondthe letter

    Old Salamano curses and beats the scabby spaniel he has come to resemble. Raymond Sintès, "warehouse-keeper", has beaten his Arab mistress, and asks Meursault to write a letter luring her back so he can punish her. Meursault writes it because he has no reason not to. "Now you're a real pal."

  • I.4The beating"it didn't mean anything"Marie

    Marie asks if he loves her. It doesn't mean anything, he says, but probably not. They hear Raymond beating the woman. A policeman comes and slaps Raymond. Meursault agrees to testify for him. That evening Salamano's dog runs away.

  • I.5One life as good as anotherParis, marriage, the dogindifference

    His boss offers him a post in Paris, and he doesn't care. Marie proposes, and it makes no difference to him, so yes, if she likes. A little "robot woman" ticks off radio programmes at Céleste's. Salamano weeps for the dog he used to beat. Meursault realises the neighbourhood judged him for sending Maman to the home.

  • I.6The beachSunday, Masson's cabinsun ●●●●●

    Marie swimming, happiness. Then two Arabs, one of them the mistress's brother. A fight: Raymond is cut. Raymond returns with a revolver, and Meursault talks him into handing it over. Later, dazed by the heat, Meursault walks back alone toward the cool spring behind the rock. The Arab is there. The same sun as the funeral, light flashing off the blade, sweat in his eyes, the sky splitting open. One shot. Then four more into the still body: "like knocking four quick times on the door of unhappiness".

  • II.1The magistratequestions, a crucifix"Antichrist"

    His lawyer is embarrassed: he probably loved Maman, "but that didn't mean anything". The examining magistrate fixates on the pause between the first shot and the other four, brandishes a silver crucifix and demands repentance. Meursault says he doesn't believe in God, and the magistrate calls him "Monsieur l'Antéchrist" from then on. Regret? "Less regret than a kind of annoyance."

  • II.2Prisoneleven monthstime dissolves

    Marie visits once, in a room full of shouting, and then can't come back because she isn't his wife. No women, no cigarettes, then habit. He inventories his old room crack by crack, and decides a man who had lived only one day could easily last a hundred years in prison. He rereads the clipping about the Czechoslovak murdered by his mother and sister. In his tin bowl he sees a face that won't smile.

  • II.3The trial, day oneJune heat, a full housewitnesses

    He feels like an intruder at his own trial. A young journalist with grey eyes watches him as if looking at himself. Witnesses: the director of the home (no tears, didn't know her age), the caretaker (coffee, cigarettes), Pérez (didn't see him cry), Céleste ("bad luck"), and Marie, made to admit the swim and the comedy the day after the funeral. His lawyer: is he accused of burying his mother or of killing a man? The prosecutor: of burying a mother "with a criminal heart".

  • II.4The trial, day twothe speeches, the verdict"because of the sun"

    The prosecutor builds premeditation out of the letter, the gun, the return and the four shots, and calls Meursault a soulless monster. He even ties him to the parricide to be tried the next day. Through the window, the ice-cream seller's trumpet. Asked to speak, Meursault says it was "because of the sun", and the room laughs. His own lawyer says "I killed", taking even his story from him. Verdict: beheading in a public square, "in the name of the French people".

  • II.5The chaplain, the night"signs and stars"revolt · calm

    He has refused the chaplain three times. He thinks of the guillotine, a machine level with the ground, and of his father, sick after an execution. Dying at thirty or seventy: no great difference. The chaplain comes anyway, "my son", God, repentance. Meursault seizes him by the collar and the book's only torrent pours out: certainty of his life and his coming death, a dark wind from the future levelling everything. Afterwards, calm. He understands why Maman took a "fiancé" at the end. He opens himself to "the gentle indifference of the world", finds it brotherly, and knows he has been happy and still is. His last wish is a large crowd at his execution, greeting him with cries of hate.

Fever chart: the sun through the book

The weather is the plot. Plot the heat and glare of each chapter and you get the story's shape: two blazing peaks (the funeral and the beach, the "same sun"), a courtroom spike in June, and a fall to the cool night of the last page.

Nº 05Characters

A constellation around a man who won't explain himself, with one dark star that never gets a name. Click a figure.

The minor figures

Thomas Pérez

Maman's lame "fiancé" at the home. He takes short cuts across the fields to keep up with the hearse and faints at the grave. In court he admits he didn't see Meursault cry, and that he didn't see much at all.

Masson & wife

Owners of the beach cabin, solid and cheerful. Their Sunday is the last ordinary happiness of Part I.

The robot woman

A small, jerky stranger at Céleste's who ticks off radio listings with mechanical precision, and later watches the trial. She is a caricature of the "unfeeling" machine the court thinks Meursault is.

Emmanuel

A colleague. They chase a lorry and jump aboard, laughing. It is pure physical joy, recorded and then forgotten.

The journalist

A young man with grey eyes who watches Meursault in court "as if looking at himself". He is often read as Camus's own cameo.

The parricide

Never seen. His case is next on the docket, and the prosecutor uses him to suggest that a son who doesn't mourn is a kind of mother-killer.

TechniqueThere is no interior monologue. Meursault reports perceptions and actions but not motives, and other people are drawn from outside (Salamano's scabs, Raymond's bandage, Marie's striped dress), almost as a camera would see them. Camus admitted borrowing this "American technique" (Hemingway, James M. Cain) to describe a man "without apparent consciousness". The outward method produces the most inward book of its decade.

Nº 06Themes & Key Takeaways

A trial of feelings, staged in the sun.

1 · The absurd

We demand meaning, and the world answers with silence. Meursault lives inside this in Part I without knowing it, becomes aware of it in prison, and accepts it on the last page.

2 · Performed feeling

He is condemned less for killing a man than for not crying, for smoking at the vigil, for a comedy at the cinema. The court punishes a breach of ritual. The trial is the real crime scene.

3 · Refusing to lie

He won't say he loves Marie, won't say he regrets, won't pretend to believe. In Camus's words, a man who "accepts death for the sake of truth", without heroics.

4 · Narrative as power

The magistrate, prosecutor and lawyer each turn chance into causes. Whoever controls the story controls the verdict, and Meursault cannot tell his life in their language.

5 · The body and the sun

Sea, sweat, salt, coffee, cigarettes, desire: the only certainties. The sun gives the book its joy and also does its killing.

6 · Death and the death penalty

It opens with a funeral and ends at the guillotine. Before certain death all lives are equally condemned, and so equally privileged. The book quietly indicts the state's cold, premeditated killing.

7 · Religion refused

A crucifix, "Antichrist", the chaplain. Meursault refuses consolation not out of rebellion but because he has no time to waste on what doesn't interest him.

8 · The invisible victim

The Arabs are nameless, faceless and silent, and the colonial court never mentions the dead man. Whether Camus exposes this or reproduces it is the central debate of later criticism.

What to carry away

  • Don't confuse expressiveness with feeling. Meursault feels heat, desire, attachment and fatigue. He just won't narrate them according to the script.
  • Watch how stories get built. The same facts become damning through sequencing and adjectives alone. It is a lesson about prosecutors, journalists and gossip.
  • Lucidity is the beginning of freedom. His revolt starts only when he looks straight at death: no hope beyond this life, so full attention to this one.
  • The present tense of the body is a kind of happiness. The book never despises its hero's pleasures.
  • A killing is never only about the killer. Read today, the book is also about who gets a name, a family and a trial in a colonial order.

TitleL'Étranger means the stranger, the foreigner and the outsider all at once. Meursault is a stranger to society's rules of feeling, to the court where his trial goes on without him, to God, and perhaps to himself. There is a colonial irony too: in Algeria, the foreigner is the European.

Nº 07Style & Craft

Sentences as islands, and a voice that changes register twice.

The island sentence

Short, declarative sentences linked by "and" and "then", almost never by "because". In Sartre's image, each sentence is an island. Events follow one another without explaining one another.

The tense

The passé composé (je suis allé) is the past tense of speech, used instead of the literary passé simple (j'allai). It breaks narration into closed, self-contained moments, the order in which a man noticed things.

Two registers: watch the rhythm change

Part I: short, separate sentences of almost equal length, each closed off. Days pass like this, with no subordinate clauses and no causes.

Voice

Detached, sensory, polite, and occasionally very funny: the old people at the vigil clicking their tongues, the robot woman, Salamano's matching scabs. Deadpan rather than cold. The narrator is not unreliable in the usual sense, because he never lies. He leaves out whatever he can't verify, and the reader, like the court, rushes to supply motives. The reader becomes a juror.

The first line, in five Englishes

One of the most debated opening sentences in translation. The French is Aujourd'hui, maman est morte. The question is one word: maman, which is what a French adult says to and about his own mother, tender and slightly childlike.

coldintimate
Which translation?Matthew Ward (1988, Vintage) keeps Maman, the short sentences and the American cadence Camus admired. Gilbert's 1946 version, standard for four decades, smoothed the flatness into British elegance and added causal links Camus left out. Sandra Smith's 2012 Penguin Outsider is fine for UK readers.

Nº 08Philosophy

Neither suicide nor hope: the third way, lived on the last page.

The novel dramatises what The Myth of Sisyphus calls the absurd. It is not the world itself that is absurd, but the divorce between our demand for meaning and the world's silence. Camus sees three exits, and closes two of them.

Ideas in dialogue

Sartre

"Explication de L'Étranger" (1943) read the novel through Sisyphus, compared it to Voltaire's tales, and named the island sentences. It made the book famous and fixed the existentialist label Camus refused. For Sartre, freedom is commitment. For Camus, the starting point is the body's happiness and the world's silence.

Nietzsche

No God, so no given values. Meursault is an innocent amor fati, a yes to the earth, without Nietzsche's will to power.

Kierkegaard & Pascal

Thinkers of the leap of faith, which Camus calls philosophical suicide. The chaplain is their heir.

Dostoevsky

"If God does not exist, everything is permitted." Camus turns it around: without God, nothing is justified, not even the court's verdict.

Kafka

The Trial's court is absurd too. But Josef K. never learns his crime. Meursault knows exactly what he did and is condemned for something else.

An inverted Passion

A crucifix, an "Antichrist", a sacrificial death, a crowd. Camus's 1955 phrase "the only Christ we deserve" is half joke, half thesis: a man who dies for the truth, with no heaven.

Where the logic strains

  • guiltInnocent or guilty? We side with him against the court. But he did kill a man, and fired four more shots into him. The book's moral magic is to make us forget the victim, which is exactly what the colonial court does. Exposure or performance?
  • valuesIs the absurd an ethic? If nothing matters, why is honesty better than lying? Meursault's sincerity is a value. Camus saw the problem and moved on to revolt and solidarity.
  • cause"Because of the sun." The novel refuses motives, yet the shooting can be read as heat, as Raymond's feud, as fate, or as a colonial reflex. The ambiguity is the point and also the limit.
  • arcPassivity vs. revolt. For most of the book he just goes along. Revolt arrives in the last pages. Some readers call it unearned; others call it the whole meaning.

The deeper claimMeaning is not given, and life is not therefore worthless. On the contrary, this life (the sun, the sea, a woman's laugh, an evening sky) is all there is, and it is enough to be happy in, if one has the courage to stop lying about it. Standing

It is one of the most read French novels anywhere, with millions of copies in Gallimard's Folio edition alone. Le Monde's 1999 poll of the "100 Books of the Century" put it at number one. The 1957 Nobel cited work that "illuminates the problems of the human conscience in our times".

Influence

The flat, affectless first-person narrator, without whom it is hard to imagine much later minimalism: Highsmith's Ripley, Bret Easton Ellis, Murata's Convenience Store Woman. "The absurd" became an everyday word, and the book became a touchstone for asking who speaks in colonial literature.

Nº 10Criticism & Counterpoints

The prosecution and the defence. Flip each charge to hear the reply.

The case against, at full strengthThe Stranger is a brilliantly engineered trick. It makes us sympathise with a man who shoots another man five times and thinks about it less than about the heat. It earns that sympathy by making the victim a non-person and his community invisible, and by staging a trial that punishes manners rather than murder. The reader is flattered into siding with the honest outsider against the hypocritical bourgeois, while the colonial hierarchy that made the killing banal is quietly reproduced. Its philosophy of indifference cannot hold to the end either: the book closes in rhetoric and a longing for the crowd.
Dated elementsThe colonial assumptions, the treatment of women (Raymond's violence goes unremarked, and Marie is drawn thinly), and the casual violence. In more secular, less ritual societies, part of the shock of a son not crying has faded too.

Nº 11Who Should Read It & How

An afternoon to read, and a lifetime to argue with.

Ideal reader

Anyone from fifteen up, especially readers drawn to philosophy, readers who distrust sentimentality, and readers interested in colonial history.

Prerequisites

None. Knowing a little about French Algeria (1830–1962) enriches a second reading. Read The Myth of Sisyphus after the novel, not before.

A reading plan

  • 1Read it fast, once, as a story, in one or two sittings.
  • 2Read Part II with Part I open beside it, and watch every fact get retold in court.
  • 3Track the weather: sun, heat and light are the real plot.
  • 4Count the names. How many times is the Arab named? (Never.)
  • 5Read the last chapter aloud. Its rhythm is the transformation.
  • 6Then read Daoud, the most powerful critical reading of the book, and a fine novel in its own right.
