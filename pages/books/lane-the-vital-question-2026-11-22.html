In brief

Every living cell is powered in the same odd way. It pumps protons to one side of a membrane and lets them back through a motor. Nick Lane thinks this explains the two greatest puzzles in biology.

Where life began. In alkaline vents on the sea floor, such gradients of protons exist by nature. Life learned there to live on them, and cells have carried the arrangement with them ever since. If so, simple life should be common wherever there’s rock, water and carbon dioxide.

Why complexity came once. A bacterium’s power is tied to its surface, so it can’t afford to grow complicated. One cell escaped by taking in others, which became mitochondria. From that event Lane derives the nucleus, sex, two sexes, ageing and death.

It’s a bold theory, beautifully joined up. Some of it is well supported and some is strongly disputed, and this edition tries to mark which is which.

The problem

What complex cells are, why that is odd, and the strange power on which all cells run.

1

The black hole at the heart of biology

Introduction and Chapter 1

We don’t know why complex cells arose only once in four billion years, or why they share so many traits that bacteria lack.

If natural selection favours complicated structures, one expects to see them arise repeatedly, as eyes and wings did. Complex cells did not. Every plant, animal, fungus and alga descends from one ancestor that already had a nucleus, mitochondria, sex, straight chromosomes, introns and an internal skeleton. No surviving cell has half of the set. Organisms once thought to be intermediates, the archezoa, turned out to have had mitochondria and lost them. So either the intermediates were outcompeted to the last one, though their simplified lookalikes thrive today, or the passage from simple to complex went through a bottleneck so narrow that it has been made only once. Lane takes the second view. A bottleneck suggests a constraint, something that holds bacteria back however long they have, and he sets out to find it.

The numbers
Life appears to have begun around 4 billion years ago. The last common ancestor of complex cells lived perhaps 1.5 to 2 billion years ago. In the interval, and since, bacteria and archaea have stayed small and structurally simple.
Since 2015
In 2022 Thiomargarita magnifica was described, a bacterium about a centimetre long, visible to the naked eye, with DNA in membrane-bound packets. It has several hundred thousand genome copies, which fits Lane’s account of how giant bacteria must cope.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Bacteria are primitive.” Chemically they are far more inventive than we are. It is in form that they stand still.
2

Form and chemistry

Chapter 1

Bacteria and archaea explored almost every chemical way to live. Eukaryotes explored form. Neither did much of the other.

Lane describes life on a planetary scale. Bacteria invented photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation and every sort of respiration, and altered the oceans, the air and the rocks. By contrast an oak, a mushroom and a person look as different as living things can, yet under an electron microscope their cells are hard to tell apart and their energy metabolism is the same. This is odd if complexity is a response to ways of life. Plants and fungi have scarcely anything in common in how they live, and one might expect each way of life to have produced its own kind of cell. The shared machinery can’t have been an adaptation to a shared way of living. Lane suspects that it was a response to something internal, common to all of them, that happened at the start: the arrival of one cell in another.

Example
Some microbes respire rock, sulphur, nitrate or even concrete. Human cells have one main trick, which our mitochondria brought with them.
Since 2015
Surveys keep adding microbial lineages, including archaea with many proteins once thought peculiar to eukaryotes (see concept 15). Their cells, as far as they have been seen, are small.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Evolution leads naturally to complexity.” Four billion years of bacteria suggest the default is staying simple.

Why oxygen isn’t the whole answer

Chapter 1

The textbook view that rising oxygen permitted complexity explains too little.

The Great Oxidation came about 2.4 billion years ago, and another rise preceded the animals. It is tempting to see oxygen as the trigger and to treat the rest as filling in. Lane argues that an environmental trigger makes the wrong prediction. It should have acted on many lineages at once. Photosynthetic bacteria would have become one kind of complex alga, osmotrophic bacteria would have become fungi, predatory ones would have become like amoebae, each by its own route and leaving its own intermediates. What we see is a single trunk with every branch sharing the same complicated equipment. A structural constraint, on the other hand, predicts exactly that: a long wait, one breakthrough by an unlikely means, and then a rapid radiation. The contrast between a permissive environment and a lifted constraint runs through the whole book.

Example
If oxygen opened up many new niches, many groups of bacteria should have moved into them independently and become complex in different ways.
Since 2015
Asgard archaea, close relatives of our host ancestor, include lineages that appear to use or tolerate oxygen, and the role of oxygen in the first partnership is being debated again.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Lane says oxygen doesn’t matter.” It matters for size and activity, especially in animals. It doesn’t explain why complexity has a single origin.
4

Living, entropy and flow

Chapter 2

Being alive is a process, a reaction kept far from equilibrium by a continuous flow of energy and matter.

Schrödinger asked what life is and answered with a code and with something he called negative entropy. Lane prefers to ask what living is. The order in a cell isn’t as costly as it looks: lipids fall into membranes by themselves and proteins fold spontaneously, because doing so releases water to a greater disorder. The real cost is in making the building blocks, joining them, and replacing them without end, and it is met by a main reaction running alongside. Life, on this view, is a side-effect of a chemical reaction that the environment wants to happen, such as hydrogen with carbon dioxide or food with oxygen. A cell gives that reaction a way to proceed and takes a share. This makes the question about origins much more definite. Don’t begin by asking how information arose. Ask where on the early Earth there was a steady, powerful disequilibrium with a structure that could make use of it.

Example
A living cell and one that died a moment ago hold the same information in the same structures. What has stopped is the flow.
The numbers
Gram for gram, a resting person converts about 2 milliwatts. The Sun averages about 0.0002 milliwatts per gram. On that measure you outshine it by a factor of 10,000.
Since 2015
The thermodynamic view has been developed by many others; it remains a framework more than a tested theory of origins.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Life defies the second law.” Organisms become ordered by releasing a greater quantity of heat and waste. Total entropy rises.

5

The proton-motive force

Chapter 2

Cells store energy by pumping protons across a membrane, and tap it by letting them back through. Peter Mitchell called this chemiosmosis.

Biochemists of the 1950s hunted for a high-energy chemical linking respiration to the making of ATP. Mitchell said that there wasn’t one. Respiration, he proposed, pushes protons to one side of a membrane, and the link is the resulting difference in charge and concentration: the proton-motive force, like water held behind a dam. The idea was so unlike chemistry as then practised that it met two decades of resistance. Lane’s interest lies in its oddity. Nobody reasoning from first principles would have expected it. Why should all life on Earth charge membranes to power itself? The advantages are real. A gradient can add up amounts of energy too small to make one ATP, as small change adds up to a note, so that no energy source is too weak to use. But usefulness to modern cells doesn’t tell you where it came from. As ancient and as universal as the genetic code, it looks like a clue to where life began.

The numbers
A potential of about 150 millivolts across a membrane some 5 nanometres thick is a field of 30 million volts per metre, comparable with a bolt of lightning.
Since 2015
Nothing has weakened the universality of ion gradients. Cells that live by fermentation alone seem to be derived from others, and even they use ATP to keep up a gradient.
A common misreading
Misreading
“ATP is the fundamental energy of life.” ATP is the currency. The proton gradient is the power station.

6

The chain and the turbine

Chapter 2

Electrons hop along a chain of protein complexes to oxygen, and the energy pumps protons out. They return through ATP synthase, a rotating motor.

Lane quotes the line, usually credited to Albert Szent-Györgyi, that life is nothing but an electron looking for a place to rest. Food gives up electrons. They pass through clusters of iron and sulphur, each a little keener to hold them than the last, separated by distances so short that the electrons cross by quantum tunnelling. At the end oxygen takes them and becomes water. At three points the fall in energy drives protons across the inner membrane of the mitochondrion. Complex I, the first of these engines, is a giant with some 45 parts in mammals, and its clusters look like flakes of mineral. That resemblance matters to Lane. Iron–sulphur minerals occur in the walls of alkaline vents. He thinks that the electron chain is geology taken inside and improved. The motor is as universal as the ribosome and found in both bacteria and archaea, so the ancestor of all life very probably had one, and had a use for it.

The numbers
A turn of a motor with a ten-part rotor takes ten protons and makes three ATP. You make something like 60 to 100 kilograms of ATP a day, by recycling a small stock.
Since 2015
Cryo-electron microscopy has revealed the complexes in near-atomic detail and shown the rotor in successive positions.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The motor is a metaphor.” It physically turns, and the rotation has been filmed.

Six things a cell needs

Chapter 3

A continuous supply of reactive carbon; a supply of free energy; catalysts; a way of removing waste; compartments; and hereditary material.

The list is a filter. Suppose a replicator formed by luck in a warm pond. It would use up its ingredients and halt, unless concentrated ingredients kept coming. Lane points out how much must flow. In a modern cell the mass of waste far exceeds the mass of new cell; for each gram made, tens of grams may have to pass through. So a plausible birthplace must bring in carbon and energy in quantity, pass them over catalysts, keep what’s useful and flush the rest away. It must do this for thousands of years or more. He calls for natural structures that work as flow reactors: porous, with walls and through-flow. The filter rejects many famous settings and leaves few. His own candidate, of course, passes it, and an opponent would say that it was made to. The list is still useful to anyone, because each item can be turned into a test.

Example
Waste removal sounds dull. If products build up, reactions stop or run backwards, so it is as necessary as food.
Since 2015
Most laboratories now accept that chemistry, energy and compartments must be considered together, though they disagree on the setting.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Once you have RNA you have life.” Copying RNA requires a torrent of building blocks and energy, and an exit for the by-products.
8

Why not soup?

Chapter 3

Lane’s case against the primordial soup, ultraviolet light and lightning as sources of life’s chemistry.

A soup at equilibrium does nothing. Its molecules have already fallen to rest, or react as readily towards tar as towards anything alive. Lightning and ultraviolet deliver energy in violent, occasional bursts that break large molecules as fast as small ones are made. Lane makes an argument from continuity. Biochemistry is conservative, so the first metabolism ought to resemble some of the oldest pathways in cells, which use carbon dioxide and hydrogen, metal catalysts, thioesters, phosphates and proton gradients. None of these requires ultraviolet light. His opponents answer that the first chemistry need not have resembled what came after, and that cyanide chemistry yields nucleotides where hydrogen and carbon dioxide have not, or not convincingly. The quarrel is partly about method: do you work forward from good chemistry, or backward from life? Lane works backward.

Example
Cyanide made by lightning or ultraviolet would be dilute in the open ocean, and no living cell uses any of these as its energy source today.
Since 2015
The rival programme has advanced impressively. John Sutherland’s group derived precursors of RNA, proteins and lipids from cyanide and ultraviolet light (2015), and others favour wet and dry cycles at hot springs on land (Deamer and Damer). Neither side has persuaded the other.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The Miller–Urey experiment failed.” It made amino acids. Lane’s point is that making ingredients isn’t keeping a reaction going.
9

Alkaline hydrothermal vents

Chapter 3

Warm springs on the sea floor, made by water reacting with rock, whose fluids are alkaline and full of hydrogen and whose chimneys are mazes of tiny pores.

The process is serpentinisation. Sea water seeps into rock containing olivine, a mineral rich in iron and magnesium. The iron is oxidised and hydrogen is released, the fluid becomes strongly alkaline, and the rock swells, cracks and lets in more water. No volcano is needed. Where the warm fluid rises into the sea, minerals come out of solution and build chimneys riddled with interconnected pores about the size of cells. Lane stresses features that fit the filter. The pores are compartments. Fluid passes through without stopping. In the early ocean, which held dissolved iron, their walls would have contained iron sulphides, which are catalysts. Heat flow along narrow pores can even concentrate molecules such as nucleotides thousands of times, by thermophoresis. These vents last for tens of thousands of years or longer, and four billion years ago, on a sea floor made of suitable rock, they should have been common.

Example
Lost City, in the Atlantic, discovered on 4 December 2000. Its tallest structure, Poseidon, rises some 60 metres.
The numbers
Its fluids are at about 40 to 90°C with a pH of 9 to 11. Black smokers are far hotter, around 350°C, acidic and short-lived.
Since 2015
In 2017 NASA’s Cassini reported molecular hydrogen in the plume of Saturn’s moon Enceladus, probably made by water acting on hot rock, the same general process.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Life began in Lost City.” Lost City is a modern analogue, in an ocean that now has oxygen, less carbon dioxide and little dissolved iron.

A natural proton gradient

Chapter 3

Alkaline vent fluid inside, acid ocean outside, a thin mineral wall between: a proton gradient with the same direction and size as that of a living cell.

Here geology and biochemistry meet. Hydrogen and carbon dioxide ought to react and release energy, yet under ordinary conditions nothing happens. There’s a first step, an uphill one. Methanogens and acetogens, which live by this reaction, climb the hill with the help of a proton gradient. Lane’s suggestion is that vents offered the gradient for nothing. Both molecules’ readiness to react depends on acidity, in opposite senses. Put hydrogen in alkaline water and carbon dioxide in acid water, separate them with a thin conducting wall of iron sulphide, and the electrons have somewhere to go. The gradient in question is three or four pH units, comparable in power with that across a cell’s membrane. If the argument is right, the strange habit of life isn’t an invention at all. Cells learned to keep up for themselves, at great expense, what the first vents had provided. Whether walls in real vents were thin enough, and the gradients steep enough across them, is the point most attacked by critics.

The numbers
Ocean at about pH 6, vent fluid at about pH 10. Reducing carbon dioxide gets easier as pH falls, and hydrogen is a stronger reducer as pH rises. At pH 7 on both sides hydrogen (−414 millivolts) can’t push electrons onto carbon dioxide to make formaldehyde (about −580). With hydrogen at pH 10 (about −590) and carbon dioxide at pH 6 (about −520), it can.
Since 2015
In 2020 Hudson, Sojo, Lane and colleagues reported formate made from hydrogen and carbon dioxide at room temperature across iron–nickel–sulphide precipitates, driven by a pH gradient; without the gradient, the hydrogen or the precipitate they detected no product. Others produced formate, acetate and pyruvate with native metals. Against this, J. Baz Jackson (2016) argued that the natural gradients were unlikely to have been usable.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The vents supplied ATP.” They supplied a disequilibrium. Making use of it required structure.

The acetyl CoA pathway and LUCA

Chapter 3 and Chapter 4

The simplest known way of making organic matter from carbon dioxide, and the only one that releases energy overall. It is found in both bacteria and archaea, and so may go back to LUCA, the last universal common ancestor.

Most ways of fixing carbon cost energy, so a cell must have an energy supply before it can grow. The acetyl CoA pathway is different. It is short and linear, relies on iron, nickel and sulphur at its key steps, and pays its own way: as Everett Shock put it, a free lunch you’re paid to eat. From acetyl CoA, chemistry leads to sugars, lipids and amino acids, and from its relative acetyl phosphate there may be a route to something doing the work of ATP. Lane sketches a path by which vent chemistry could become metabolism. Simple organics form on the walls, accumulate in pores, spontaneously make leaky vesicles, and improve the catalysis that made them, so that a kind of positive feedback begins before genes. He admits that the details are missing here. What he claims is direction, and that the starting materials and catalysts of the oldest pathway are those of the vents.

Example
Acetogens (bacteria) and methanogens (archaea) live on hydrogen and carbon dioxide alone.
Since 2015
Weiss and colleagues (2016) traced 355 protein families to LUCA and inferred an organism living on hydrogen and carbon dioxide in a setting rich in iron. Moody and colleagues (2024) dated LUCA to about 4.2 billion years ago and gave it some 2,600 proteins, an early immune system and neighbours. That is a more sophisticated creature than Lane pictured.
A common misreading
Misreading
“LUCA was the first cell.” It was the latest ancestor shared by everything now living, with a long history behind it.
12

Leaky membranes

Chapter 4

The first cells must have had membranes that leaked protons. Only leaky cells could live on a natural gradient.

Imagine a vesicle lodged at the boundary of an acid and an alkaline stream. Protons enter on the acid side through a primitive motor and do work. If the membrane is tight they can’t leave, they gather within, and in moments the gradient is gone. If it leaks, hydroxide from the alkaline side gets in and neutralises them, so the flow continues indefinitely. So the cell depends on leakiness. This produces a paradox. To leave the vents, cells must make gradients for themselves, which takes pumps and tight membranes. But pumping through a leaky membrane is like pumping up a tyre with a hole in it, and tightening the membrane first cuts off the natural supply. Each step towards freedom is a step down. The model makes this quantitative, and the next concept is Lane’s way out.

The numbers
In the model by Sojo, Pomiankowski and Lane (2014), leaky cells in a three-unit gradient kept most of the available power. Making membranes as tight as modern ones collapsed it. Pumping brought no advantage even when leakiness fell a thousandfold.
Since 2015
Jordan, Lane and colleagues (2019) showed that mixtures of simple fatty acids and alcohols form vesicles in alkaline, salty water at around 70°C, against the usual objection that they can’t.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Leakiness is a defect.” With a natural flow on both sides, a leak lets protons that have entered get out again and be neutralised.
13

The antiporter and the great divide

Chapter 4

A simple protein that swaps protons for sodium ions gave leaky cells more power and made it worthwhile to evolve tight membranes, and they did so twice: once in bacteria, once in archaea.

Membranes are much leakier to protons than to sodium ions. So if a protein trades one for the other, using the natural gradient of protons to push sodium out, the cell acquires a second gradient that doesn’t leak away. It’s better off at once. It can spread to parts of the vent where the gradient is weaker. And now, for the first time, pumping pays, because some of what is pumped stays pumped, and each tightening of the membrane brings a further gain. Lane suggests that this took place in two populations in different parts of a vent system. Each hit upon its own solution to the same difficulty: its own lipids, its own wall, its own way of pumping, and, since DNA copying is tied to the membrane, its own machinery for that. The one power source explains both what bacteria and archaea have in common and where they differ. It’s the book’s most elegant stretch of reasoning. It depends on the natural-gradient premise at every step.

Example
Bacterial membrane lipids are built on one mirror-image form of glycerol phosphate, and archaeal lipids on the other.
The numbers
In the model the sodium–proton antiporter added about 60 per cent to the power available and allowed survival in gradients 50 times weaker.
Since 2015
Bacteria with genes for both lipid types have been reported, and engineered cells with mixed membranes proved stable. That shows mixtures are possible and makes the late, separate origin of tight membranes one explanation among several.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Bacteria and archaea had separate origins.” They share the genetic code, ribosomes and ATP synthase. They differ in membranes, cell walls and parts of the copying of DNA.

Complexity

Why bacteria stayed simple, and what one of them did instead.

14

Energy per gene

Chapter 5

What limits a cell’s complexity is the energy it has available for each of its genes. Bacteria can’t get big without losing out; complex cells, with mitochondria, can.

Expressing a gene is expensive, and nearly all of the cost is in making proteins, not DNA. A cell makes its power at a membrane. In bacteria the membrane is the cell’s own skin. So power grows with the square of the diameter while the volume to be supplied grows with the cube. Folding the membrane inwards seems an obvious remedy. Lane says that it doesn’t work, because respiration needs genes close by to regulate it, so more membrane needs more copies of the genome. Giant bacteria bear him out: they have thousands of copies, distributed beneath the surface, around a mostly inert interior, and, per gene, no more energy than a small one. Mitochondria break the bond. They carry membranes wherever they’re needed with a tiny remnant genome each, instead of a full one. The saved energy can be spent on making more protein from a large nuclear genome. Scale, gene number and internal structure become affordable. The argument explains a great deal from simple geometry, and the dispute is mostly about whether the geometry really bites at the sizes that matter.

The numbers
Enlarge a bacterium 25 times in diameter: its surface grows 625 times and its volume 15,625 times. Lane estimates that complex cells have around 5,000 times more energy per gene than an average bacterium, and 125,000 times more than one scaled up to their size. The 2010 paper speaks of a 200,000-fold expansion in the genes that could be expressed.
Since 2015
The most contested claim in the book. Lynch and Marinov (2017) concluded that eukaryotes aren’t energetically better off and that mitochondria had no direct causal role. Schavemaker and Muñoz-Gómez (2022) found the advantage modest at the sizes likely for the first eukaryotes, and argued that mitochondria mattered later, for larger and faster-dividing cells. Lane and Martin have defended their view.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Mitochondria are more efficient.” They respire in the same way that bacteria do. What differs is the arrangement of membranes and genes.

15

One cell inside another

Chapter 5

The eukaryotic cell began as a partnership between two simple cells: an archaeon as host, and a bacterium that became the mitochondrion.

Two schools explain complexity. In one, a lineage gradually acquired a nucleus, skeleton and the ability to swallow prey, and then swallowed the bacterium that became the mitochondrion. In the other, the partnership came first and the rest followed from it. Lane holds to the second. He has three grounds. All eukaryotes have, or once had, mitochondria. The genes of eukaryotes look like a chimera, with information-handling genes from archaea and many metabolic ones from bacteria. And his energy argument says that complexity can’t be afforded without mitochondria. Cells of this sort do sometimes contain others: there are bacteria living inside bacteria in mealybugs. But prokaryotes have no means of engulfing, so entry must be very rare, and a lasting settlement far rarer. That rarity is what he needs, to explain an event that happened once. The cost of the theory is that so much hangs on it.

Example
Martin and Müller’s hydrogen hypothesis: the bacterium gave off hydrogen as waste, and the archaeon lived on hydrogen.
Since 2015
Strong support for the archaeal host. Lokiarchaeota were announced in the month after the book’s British publication, and the wider Asgard group contains many eukaryote-like proteins. Imachi and colleagues (2020) grew one, Prometheoarchaeum: small, slow, living in partnership, with long branching protrusions that could entangle a neighbour. Whether mitochondria came early or late remains disputed.
A common misreading
Misreading
“A large predatory cell swallowed a bacterium.” On Lane’s account the host wasn’t complex or a predator. It was an ordinary archaeon without any machinery for engulfing.
16

Why mitochondria kept their genes

Chapter 5

Almost all the bacterium’s genes were lost or moved to the nucleus. A handful stayed because respiration needs a local control.

John Allen’s idea goes by the ungainly name of co-location for redox regulation. A respiratory chain works at a fearsome voltage, and if electrons back up they escape and make destructive free radicals. The chain must be adjusted minute by minute by adding new parts, and the adjustment has to be specific: this mitochondrion needs more of complex IV, and its neighbour doesn’t. If all the genes sat in the nucleus, the response would be sent to every mitochondrion alike. A small local genome solves the problem. Lane takes the same principle in both directions. It explains why a cell can’t have large internal membranes without genes to go with them, hence the failure of giant bacteria. And it explains the great economy of mitochondria, which keep just what’s needed for control. Thousands of copies of something tiny cost far less than thousands of copies of something large.

The numbers
Human mitochondrial DNA has 37 genes, 13 of which code for proteins, all of them parts of the respiratory machinery.
Since 2015
Analyses of thousands of mitochondrial genomes support several factors, including local control and the difficulty of importing very water-repelling proteins. A salmon parasite, Henneguya, was reported in 2020 to have lost its mitochondrial genome, and the single-celled Monocercomonoides was shown in 2016 to have lost mitochondria altogether. Neither respires with oxygen.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The remaining genes are leftovers that haven’t moved yet.” The same small core has been kept independently in lineage after lineage.
17

Introns and the nucleus

Chapter 5

Eukaryotic genes are broken up by introns. Lane and Martin argue that these came from parasitic DNA in the mitochondrial ancestor, and that the nucleus evolved to give time for removing them.

Bacteria often carry mobile group II introns, scraps of selfish DNA that copy themselves into genomes and then cut themselves out of the RNA. In large bacterial populations selection keeps them few. Now put such a bacterium inside an archaeon. When one of the resident cells dies its DNA spills into the host, and the introns invade a genome with no defences. They multiplied, then decayed, until they could no longer cut themselves out, and their broken parts became the cutting machine we call the spliceosome. It was slow. In a bacterium ribosomes begin reading an RNA while it’s still being made, so anything slower than a ribosome is too late, and unspliced RNA yields nonsense. One remedy is distance: keep the ribosomes away from the DNA until the cutting is over. On this view the nucleus began as a refuge from a bombardment. It’s a good example of Lane’s method, in which a grand feature is the repair of an internal accident.

The numbers
Ribosomes add around ten amino acids a second and can finish a typical bacterial protein in under half a minute. Cutting out an intron may take minutes.
Since 2015
The argument (Martin and Koonin, 2006) remains a leading hypothesis. The relation between the spliceosome and bacterial mobile introns has been confirmed structurally. The nuclear pore’s origin is still unclear.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The nucleus protects the DNA.” A barrier full of large holes isn’t much of a wall. Its effect is to separate copying from reading.

Splicing is slow. How much of a head start does it need before the ribosomes arrive? Each square is a message.

18

The chimera

Chapter 5

Eukaryotic cells are archaeal in their handling of information and bacterial in their membranes and much of their metabolism. The host’s own membranes were replaced.

A branching tree can’t show what happened here, because two branches joined. Genes that arrived from the bacterium went to the nucleus and their products were sent wherever they proved useful. Among them were genes for making bacterial lipids. Lane supposes that vesicles of bacterial lipid simply gathered in the host, as the product was made and had nowhere special to go, and so internal membranes appeared, including those that came to surround the DNA. With time they displaced the host’s own. He also reminds us that gene transfer runs in one direction. The mitochondria are many and the nucleus is one; a mitochondrion may die and release DNA, and the host lives on to inherit it, whereas a host that bursts leaves no one to inherit. So a ratchet moves genes towards the nucleus. Much that looks like the host’s achievement is thus the guest’s dowry, spent by the household.

Example
Every eukaryote has bacterial-type lipids, though its host ancestor was an archaeon.
Since 2015
Most analyses with better data place eukaryotes within or beside the Asgard archaea, favouring the two-domain tree. How the lipids were replaced isn’t known.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The tree of life simply has three branches.” If the host arose within the archaea, there are two primary domains and eukaryotes are a fusion of branches.

Sex and death

What follows when two kinds of cell must live as one.

19

Why sex

Chapter 6

Mixing genes lets selection act on each gene separately. Without it, good and bad mutations are stuck together and large genomes decay.

In a clone a whole genome is selected as a unit. A beneficial mutation sweeps to success and takes its neighbours with it, harmful ones included, and two good mutations in different individuals compete instead of combining. In small populations the best-endowed class is lost by chance, time after time, and can’t be rebuilt, a process called Muller’s ratchet. Lane applies this to the first eukaryotes. They were few, they were under attack by introns, and their genomes were growing. Lateral transfer wouldn’t save them. They needed reciprocal exchange across whole chromosomes, again and again: that is, sex. The conditions of their birth, he says, made it necessary. They may also have made it possible, if cells without walls fused easily and if mitochondria, seeking new hosts, gained by the fusions. He is most convincing on why sex is indispensable to anything with a large genome, and more tentative about how it began.

Example
Bacteria shuffle genes too, by lateral transfer, but only in short pieces, which serves a small genome and can’t maintain a big one.
Since 2015
Work at UCL with Marco Colnaghi and Andrew Pomiankowski has modelled the limits of lateral transfer as genomes grow, supporting this view. The evolution of sex still has several competing accounts.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Sex exists for variety.” Variety in itself often breaks up good combinations. The advantage lies in letting selection see genes one by one.

Model 9

The ratchet

Two populations gather harmful mutations. One clones itself. The other mixes its genes. Both are under the same selection.

harmful mutations, clones
harmful mutations, sexual

Each mutation lowers fitness by 4%. Sex halves each parent’s load at random and adds them. Small populations show the ratchet best; in large ones it slows.

20

Two sexes

Chapter 6

Mitochondria pass down through one parent. Lane’s group argues that this improves the fit between mitochondrial and nuclear genes by increasing the variation between cells, for selection to act on.

Cells contain many mitochondria. If both parents contribute, each daughter gets a blend, and blending hides differences: every cell is a mixture of good and bad. If one parent contributes, the number of sources is halved, and by chance some cells come out mostly good and some mostly bad. Now selection has something to choose between. Over generations bad mitochondria are purged and the survivors are those that work well with the nuclear genes. So the distinction between the sexes, on this account, starts with a difference in who passes on mitochondria, and the eggs and sperm, the ornaments and the quarrels came later. The older view had been that uniparental inheritance prevents conflict between rival mitochondria. Lane doesn’t deny conflict. He thinks that quality control is the steadier reason, and his colleagues’ models show that the advantage tends to shrink as uniparental inheritance spreads, which may explain why nature has so many partial arrangements.

Example
In almost all animals mitochondria come from the egg. A sperm’s are excluded or destroyed.
Since 2015
Models by Hadjivasiliou, Radzvilavicius and others in the group found that the benefit is real but limited, and that different tissues and life histories call for different rules. Exceptions, such as biparental inheritance in some mussels, are known.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Two sexes are needed for mixing genes.” Any two individuals could do that. With one sex everyone could mate with everyone. Two halves your choice.

Model 10

Parents’ fractions are uniform from 0 to 100%; each cell gets 24 mitochondria. Two parents average out, so selection has less to work on.

21

The germline and the body

Chapter 6

Animals set aside eggs early and protect them. The rest of the body can be used up. That is why bodies die.

Mitochondrial mutations gather with each round of copying. Why, then, don’t they ruin us within a few generations? In animals such as ourselves an egg’s mitochondria have been through few rounds, and have been kept quiet. Making many eggs and letting most of them die gives selection further chances. Sperm, which must be made continuously and swim hard, are excused from passing on mitochondria. Plants and simple animals have a lower rate of mitochondrial mutation and can afford to make germ cells late from ordinary tissue; indeed selection among cells during growth serves them well. Once there is a protected line, the rest of the body becomes disposable. It can specialise to any degree, with brains of irreplaceable neurons, because none of it has to last. Lane is eloquent on this: complicated bodies and certain death were purchased together, and a brain is the sort of thing only a mortal creature can have.

Example
Plants and sponges have no early germline. A cutting or a fragment can become a whole.
The numbers
A human egg has several hundred thousand copies of mitochondrial DNA, formed before birth and largely kept at rest. A sperm has at most about a hundred.
Since 2015
Radzvilavicius, Lane and Pomiankowski developed this as a formal model (2016). Germline sequestration remains only partly understood.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Death is programmed for the good of the species.” Lane follows the standard view, in which natural selection loses interest in the old. He adds the part played by mitochondria.

Predictions

If the theory is right, these things should be so.

22

The mitonuclear mosaic

Chapter 7

Respiratory complexes are built from proteins made by two genomes, nuclear and mitochondrial, which must fit to within fractions of a nanometre.

The two genomes evolve under different rules. In animals mitochondrial genes mutate ten to fifty times faster, pass down one line and don’t recombine. Nuclear genes change slowly and are shuffled every generation. Yet their products interlock in the same complexes, passing electrons over distances at which a tiny displacement matters a great deal. So they must keep adapting to each other. When they fail to match, electrons pile up, free radicals leak, the machinery comes apart, and the signal for cell death follows. It is strange design. No engineer would draw up plans for a precision engine in two offices that used different languages and only met once a generation. From this strangeness Lane draws the rest of the chapter. If life depends on this fit, then the checking of it should leave traces in how species form, how fertile animals are and how long they live.

Example
Crossing populations of the copepod Tigriopus californicus gives offspring with weakened respiration. Backcrossing to the maternal line, so that nuclear and mitochondrial genes match again, restores it.
Since 2015
Mitonuclear interaction has become a busy field, with evidence from flies, mice, birds and human cell lines. Effects are often small, and depend on diet and environment.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Mitochondrial genes hardly matter because there are only thirteen.” Every ATP made by respiration depends on them.
23

Speciation and Haldane’s rule

Chapter 7

Mitonuclear mismatch may help to make hybrids unfit and so split species, and may explain why the sex that fails first is often male.

J. B. S. Haldane noticed in 1922 that when hybrids of one sex are absent, rare or sterile, it is the sex with two different sex chromosomes: XY males in mammals, ZW females in birds. Lane suggests that metabolic rate is part of the cause. The sex with the greater energy demand will be the first to fail when the respiratory machinery is poorly matched. Mammalian males, he argues, have the higher rate from early development on, and he even connects this with the way that sex is determined, by growth rate as much as by genes. The suggestion is ingenious and makes unfamiliar facts hang together. It’s also one of the places in which a reader feels the theory stretching to take in everything. Lane signals as much: the chapter is called a set of predictions.

Example
In mammals hybrid males are typically the sterile or missing ones, as with mules.
Since 2015
Geoffrey Hill and others have promoted a “mitonuclear compatibility” view of species. It remains one mechanism among several.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Lane has explained Haldane’s rule.” In birds and butterflies the sex that suffers is the female, the one with unlike chromosomes. The standard account by sex chromosomes covers both, and his needs help.
24

The death threshold

Chapter 7

Each species has a threshold of free-radical leak at which cells (or embryos) are eliminated. Where it is set trades fertility against athletic power.

Suppose that an embryo’s cells test their respiration and die if too many free radicals leak. A strict test eliminates many embryos and leaves only well-matched ones, with high aerobic capacity and little leak. A lax test leaves more embryos and includes poorer matches. Which is better depends on the way of life. A flying animal can’t do with a mediocre engine; a rat can. So strictness should go along with low fecundity, high fitness and long life, and laxity with the reverse. The scheme links four things often studied separately: fertility, adaptability, aerobic capacity and lifespan. It implies that a strict species will be slow to adapt to new conditions, because it discards variants that are only slightly mismatched. Lane speculates that a good share of early human miscarriages may reflect such a test. This is a hypothesis about populations and it isn’t a guide for any individual.

Example
Pigeons and bats, which need high power for flight, should have a low threshold: few embryos pass, and those that do are fit and long-lived. Rats should have a high one: many young, less fit, with shorter lives.
Since 2015
The trade-off remains mostly a hypothesis. Its human application, to early miscarriage, is untested and should be treated with care.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Low fertility means ill health.” On this view it may be the price of demanding standards.

25

Free radicals and ageing

Chapter 7

The old theory that ageing is damage by free radicals, to be cured by antioxidants, is wrong. Free radicals are signals that respiration needs attention.

The free-radical theory was simple, and it yielded a tidy prediction: animals that breathe faster should die sooner, and antioxidants should delay it. Birds break the first part and trials break the second. Lane reinterprets the signals. A modest leak tells a mitochondrion that its chain lacks parts, and the local genes respond by making more; the leak falls, and all is well. If the parts don’t fit, the leak continues, and in the end the cell is removed by apoptosis, with the mitochondrial protein cytochrome c released as the signal. Lost cells aren’t always replaced, and tissues decline. Add antioxidants and you may suppress the very signal that would have prompted the repair. So lifespan should be connected with how well the two genomes work together and with how rapidly the respiratory machinery is renewed, and exercise, which stimulates renewal, ought to be better than pills. The last point agrees with a good deal of evidence obtained for other reasons.

Example
Large clinical trials of antioxidant supplements have found no extension of life, and some found harm.
The numbers
A pigeon and a rat have similar weight and resting metabolic rate. The pigeon lives around ten times as long, and its mitochondria leak fewer free radicals.
Since 2015
The signalling role is widely accepted. Ageing is now treated as having many interlocking causes, of which mitochondria are one.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Free radicals are harmless.” In excess they kill cells. The point is that swamping them can silence an alarm.
26

Life elsewhere

Chapter 7 and Epilogue

Bacteria, or something like them, should be common in the universe. Complex life should be rare.

The two halves of the theory point in opposite directions. The first says that life grows out of planetary chemistry. Olivine is among the commonest minerals in the universe, water is everywhere, and carbon dioxide is usual in the atmospheres of rocky planets. So vents, and cells running on proton gradients, ought to be the rule: the universe should be full of bacteria, and if we find them we should expect them to be charged. The second says that going further needs a cell to get inside another, with no means of doing so, and for the two to come to terms over thousands of generations. On Earth that has occurred once that we know about. So the answer to “Are we alone?” is that we probably have an abundance of distant microbial cousins and few to talk to. Lane closes with gratitude to this accident, and with the thought that a universe able to know itself may be uncommon.

Example
Parakaryon myojinensis, one specimen found in a deep-sea worm’s bristle, has a partial nucleus and internal symbionts but doesn’t fit any known group. It may be a second try, or something else.
The numbers
Lane cites an estimate of some 40 billion Earth-like planets in the Milky Way. The requirements he lists are rock, water and carbon dioxide.
Since 2015
Hydrogen at Enceladus made the case for habitable moons stronger. No further specimen of Parakaryon has been reported, to my knowledge, and one cell can bear little weight.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Complex life must be unique to Earth.” He says improbable, and with billions of planets improbable things may occur.

Themes and takeaways

What to keep, whichever way the arguments go.

Energy constrains

Genes propose and power disposes.

Universals are clues

Whatever all cells share, however odd, records where they came from.

Novelties are repairs

The nucleus, sex and the germline are answers to internal trouble.

Two genomes, one engine

The partnership is the strength of complex life and also its weak point.

Seven to carry away

  1. Ask of any living system: where is the flow, and what is the gradient?
  2. You are a proton machine. Food is electrons, breath is where they go, and ATP is change.
  3. Bigger isn’t just more. Surface and volume scale differently, and so do costs.
  4. When history left no fossils, work backward from what everything shares.
  5. Multiply the ifs. A chain of plausible steps may not be plausible.
  6. Antioxidant pills aren’t supported by this theory or by the trials. Exercise is.
  7. A theory worth having says what would refute it.

Style and craft

A detective story told backwards from the survivors.

The prose is personal and urgent. Lane moves from a vivid comparison to hard biochemistry within a page, asks questions, retraces his steps and says when he’s delighted. Some passages on redox chemistry are demanding, and he doesn’t hide it.

Predicament and escape

His characters are cells with problems: the leaky one that can’t leave home, the host under bombardment. It makes biochemistry dramatic. It also makes inference read like history.

One constraint, many dresses

By Chapter 7 a reader can see some of the deductions coming. That is a pleasure and a warning.

The laboratory

He credits colleagues by name and describes arguments, meetings and an apparatus that leaked. Few books show so well how a theory is made.

The title

Vital means both essential and to do with life. It also nods to the old idea of a life force, which here becomes a stream of protons.

Philosophy

Simple life is close to law. Complex life is close to luck.

History is contingent, but within channels cut by physics. Lane stands between those who think life is a freak and those who think mind is inevitable. Beside Dawkins’s The Selfish Gene the book makes a good pair: Dawkins asks what persists, and Lane asks what pays for it.

Where it strains

  • A chain of ifsEach step is plausible. Multiplied together they are less so, and the story makes it easy to forget to multiply.
  • A single eventWhen something has happened once, is it a constraint, a rare accident, or a first-comer that shut the door behind it?
  • Genes, soon afterHow heredity and the code arose is the thinnest part of the account.
  • A master keyAn explanation that opens vents, sex, hybrids, fertility and ageing is thrilling, and should make one wary.
The deeper claim

Biology can be predictive about its largest questions. Given the right constraint, a great deal of what looks arbitrary in life might have been expected.

Criticism and counterpoints

Turn each card for the reply.

A litmus test

How firm is each claim now? Red is speculation, blue is established. These are my readings of the literature as I know it. Specialists would move some of them.

  • Cells run on proton gradientsNear universal, and worked out in atomic detail.established
  • Mitochondria were once bacteriaNo longer in doubt.established
  • The host was an archaeonStrengthened greatly by the Asgard archaea.well supported
  • LUCA lived on hydrogen and carbon dioxideTwo large studies agree on this much, and differ on how elaborate it was.well supported
  • Free radicals are signals; supplements don’t slow ageingWidely accepted, as part of a larger picture.well supported

  • Life began in alkaline ventsOne of two or three serious candidates.contested
  • Mitochondria were needed for complexity, because of energy per geneDisputed on the numbers.contested
  • One-parent inheritance evolved for quality controlModels show a benefit, with limits.contested

  • Complex life is rare in the universeAn inference from a sample of one.speculative
The case against, at full strength

Lane takes two unique, unobservable events and explains both by one favoured mechanism, with great narrative skill. The first explanation rests on conditions nobody can check, the second on estimates that others have recalculated and not found. Between them lie models built on the theory’s own premises. The reader feels that a case has been proved when it has been told.

The fair reply

Any account of origins must be a chain of inference, and this one produces experiments. Several predictions have done well. On energetics the argument has moved from whether mitochondria matter to when and how much.

How to read it

Patiently through Chapter 2, then with growing speed, then with a critic.

If you read nothing else

Chapter 2 for the proton-motive force. Chapter 3 for the vents. Chapter 5 for the book’s hinge. And the epilogue, short, strange and moving.

School chemistry helps. If the word redox alarms you, begin with Life Ascending.
