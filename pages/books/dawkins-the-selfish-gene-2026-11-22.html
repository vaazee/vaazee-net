Book analysis: evolution, 1976

The Selfish Gene

Bodies die and genes don’t have to. Richard Dawkins looks at life from the side of the thing that lasts, and finds there the sources of conflict, love, cheating and cooperation.

In brief

Evolution needs something that is copied, varies and lasts. You don’t last. You are one hand of cards, dealt once. The cards go back into the pack. Dawkins asks what nature looks like if we follow the cards.

From that position plants and animals are survival machines, built by cooperating genes. Some acts that look like sacrifice turn out to help copies of the same gene in another body. Some peace turns out to be merely stable. The book works out when selfish genes make selfish animals and when they make generous ones, then adds that culture may have replicators of its own, which Dawkins named memes.

“Selfish” names an effect on survival. It is not a motive, and it is not advice. The book ends by asking us to disobey.

“We, alone on earth, can rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators.”The last sentence of the original book

The author

An ethologist who thinks in analogies and argues to the end.

Training

Born in Nairobi in 1941, raised in England, a zoologist at Oxford under Niko Tinbergen, who taught him to separate how a behaviour works from what it is for.

Teaching

He returned from Berkeley to Oxford in 1970 and lectured on Hamilton’s ideas before most biologists had read them. Parts of the book began there.

Temper

Lucid, witty, sure of himself. He believes that explanation adds to wonder, and is impatient with vagueness and religion.

Reputation

Among biologists this and The Extended Phenotype are his respected works. The public knows him better for The God Delusion, and later statements on religion and gender have drawn heavy criticism.

Among his books

The first, and by most accounts the most important.

Most of its ideas are other people’s, as Dawkins says plainly. Three things were his: the clear separation of replicator and vehicle, the consistent retelling of every problem from the gene’s side, and the meme. The Extended Phenotype (1982) is the professional sequel and his own favourite; The Blind Watchmaker (1986) is the better introduction to how evolution works.

The argument

From a molecule in a soup to the trenches of 1915, with one idea in hand.

The thesis

Selection over long spans is best described as the differential survival of genes. Those we find are the ones that promoted their own copying against rival versions. So genes tend to be “selfish”, and whether the animals they build are selfish or generous depends on circumstances the book sets out.

Method

Imagine a gene for some behaviour. Ask whether it would spread. Work out the conditions with arithmetic a reader can check. Field studies come in as tests and illustrations. The 1989 endnotes then say, with unusual candour, where the text had been wrong.

The replicator

What is copied, what carries it, and how a blind copying process comes to look like design.

1

The gene’s-eye view

Chapters 1 and 2

A way of seeing evolution in which the gene, not the organism or the group, is the thing that benefits.

Dawkins opens by separating three things people run together: how we evolved, how we behave and how we ought to behave. The book concerns the first, touches the second and disclaims the third. He then defines altruism by effects, asking only whether an act lowers the actor’s survival prospects and raises another’s. Motives don’t come into it, so a virus can be selfish. The reason for choosing the gene is durability. A body is a one-off combination, like a hand of cards; the cards themselves go back into the pack and are dealt again. If you want to know what evolution accumulates over a million years, you have to look at what is still there after a million years, and that is the cards. Everything else in the book comes from taking that choice seriously.

Example
A worker bee dies when it stings. Asked what the bee gains, we are stuck. Asked what a gene for stinging gains, we can see that copies of it survive in the hive.
Since 1976
Dawkins compares the two views, gene and organism, to the two faces of a Necker cube: the same facts, seen differently. Most biologists now treat gene’s-eye and individual (inclusive fitness) accounts as largely interchangeable for many problems. Philosophers still argue about which one describes causes.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Genes have wishes.” The word is shorthand. A gene counts as selfish if its effects raise its own chance of survival against its rivals, in the way that water “seeks” its level.
2

Replicators

Chapter 2

Anything of which copies are made. The first replicator was a molecule that happened to assemble copies of itself.

Dawkins begins before biology, with a principle that applies to any matter: survival of the stable. Rocks and raindrops exist because their patterns last. Replication adds a second kind of stability. A pattern can now persist by being copied, even though each instance is brief. The first replicator needed only to arise once, and, given an ocean and hundreds of millions of years, the improbable had time to happen. After that, three things followed without any foresight. Copying was imperfect, so varieties appeared. Building blocks were limited, so varieties competed. And some varieties came by chance to protect themselves with coats of protein, or to break up their rivals. Those coats are the ancestors of bodies. In Dawkins’s most quoted image, the replicators did not die out. They go by the name of genes, and we are their survival machines.

Example
DNA today. Crystals, computer programs and chain letters have some of the same properties.
Since 1976
RNA, which can both store information and catalyse reactions, became the leading candidate for an early replicator. Some researchers argue that metabolism at mineral vents came before copying. The chemistry is still unsettled, but the logic of replication doesn’t depend on it.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Dawkins says DNA came first.” He says the opposite is possible: the first replicators may have been something else, later displaced by DNA.
3

Longevity, fecundity, fidelity

Chapter 2

The three qualities that make one replicator outdo another: how long copies last, how fast they are made and how accurately.

The three qualities trade against one another, and which of them matters most depends on the replicator. For individual DNA molecules longevity is trivial: each lasts about as long as its cell. For genes in the form of copies, longevity can be measured in millions of years. Fecundity explains why fast, sloppy copiers can dominate for a time, and fidelity explains why they can’t build anything elaborate, since each gain is blurred before it can be extended. Dawkins uses the triad again in Chapter 11 to test whether ideas qualify. A tune can be long-lived (Auld Lang Syne), highly fecund (a hit single), but its fidelity is doubtful, since every retelling alters it. The triad is one of the most portable tools in the book.

The numbers
Fidelity compounds. If each copying is 99% accurate, a lineage copied a hundred times in succession has about a 37% chance of reaching the end unaltered (0.99 to the power of 100).
Since 1976
Error-threshold theory (Manfred Eigen, developed from 1971) made the point exact: above some rate of error, a sequence of a given length can’t be maintained at all.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Selection always favours perfect copying.” Within a species, accurate copiers win; yet without occasional errors there would be no variety for selection to work on. Evolution happens in spite of replicators’ apparent interests.

4

What counts as a gene

Chapter 3

Any stretch of chromosome that is likely to last long enough, through the shuffling of generations, for selection to act on it as a unit. The definition is adapted from G. C. Williams.

Dawkins concedes that his definition is a matter of convenience. There is no sharp line between a gene and a bit of chromosome that isn’t one. It is a fading-out: the shorter the stretch, the longer it is likely to last as a unit, and the better it qualifies. A strictly accurate title, he jokes, would have had to mention both the slightly selfish big bit of chromosome and the more selfish little bit. The definition can look circular, choosing as the unit of selection whatever lasts long enough to be one. The point it makes isn’t empty, though. It excludes individuals, groups and species, because none of them is copied with enough fidelity for selection to act on them over long spans. The word “immortal” in the chapter’s title is to be taken in that sense: an individual molecule of DNA lives perhaps months, but its information may outlive mountains.

Example
A whole chromosome lasts one generation intact, then is cut and spliced by crossing-over. A short stretch may pass unbroken through thousands of generations.
The numbers
If crossing-over falls at random, a stretch of L centimorgans has roughly an e−Lg/100 chance of passing through g generations uncut. One centimorgan has about a 61% chance of lasting 50 generations. A 100-centimorgan chromosome has effectively none.
Since 1976
Molecular biology defines genes by what they encode, and the human genome holds about 20,000 that make proteins. Most traits studied are influenced by very many variants with small effects. Dawkins’s unit is a different, evolutionary one, which he later preferred to call the active germ-line replicator.
A common misreading
Misreading
“There is a gene for each trait.” A “gene for” something means only that, other things being equal, a difference in that stretch of DNA makes a difference to the trait.

5

Vehicles, or survival machines

Chapters 2, 3 and 13

The bodies in which replicators travel: built by genes, acting for their benefit, discarded each generation.

The distinction answers a question that had caused decades of confusion. “What is the unit of selection?” turns out to be two questions. One asks what survives or fails to survive as a result of selection, and the answer is replicators. The other asks what things do the surviving and breeding, so that selection can act, and the answer is vehicles. An individual is a very good vehicle: its genes share one exit to the future, through eggs or sperm, so they have the same interest in its welfare. A herd is a poor vehicle: its members leave by separate doors. This also shows that the individual organism is something to be explained, not assumed. Dawkins takes up that puzzle in the final chapter.

Example
A human being, an oak, a mushroom. They look utterly different and run on nearly identical replicating machinery.
Since 1976
David Hull proposed interactor for the unit that meets the environment as a whole. Dawkins said the replicator/vehicle distinction dissolves part of the units-of-selection debate: groups and individuals may be rival vehicles, but neither is a replicator.
A common misreading
Misreading
“We’re just robots.” Dawkins did write of robots, and regretted some reactions. He meant machines as sophisticated as any we can imagine, able to learn, predict and choose.
6

The rowing crew

Chapters 3 and 5

Genes are selected for how well they work in the company of other genes. Selfish replicators become skilled cooperators inside a shared vehicle.

A coach has many oarsmen for eight seats. He tries them in random crews, races the boats and keeps doing so. No single race tells him much: a good oarsman may sit in a bad crew. Over many races, however, good oarsmen turn up more often in winning boats. If the team communicates, an English-speaker does well in a mostly English-speaking crew and badly in a German-speaking one, though neither language is better in itself. So the selection of individuals produces harmonious teams, without selection between teams as units. Chapter 5 gives this its exact form: a gene pool becomes an evolutionarily stable set of genes, resistant to invasion. The result looks like a plan made for the good of the whole, and it comes from each gene being tested in the environment made up of the others.

Example
Genes for a carnivore’s sharp teeth flourish in a gene pool that already contains genes for a carnivore’s gut, and are useless in one full of genes for digesting grass.
Since 1976
Genes that cheat the crew, such as segregation distorters and transposable elements, are now a large field of research. Sequences derived from transposable elements make up roughly half of the human genome.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The book says genes don’t cooperate.” It says the opposite. Dawkins later remarked that The Cooperative Gene would have been an equally apt title.

7

Genes as programmers

Chapter 4

Genes can’t control behaviour moment by moment, because they act slowly. They build brains in advance and give them policies.

The central image is Fred Hoyle and John Elliot’s story A for Andromeda. A distant civilization can’t act on Earth across two hundred light years, so it sends instructions for building a machine that can act for it. Genes are in the same position. They work by making proteins, which takes months; behaviour happens in fractions of a second. So they do what chess programmers do: they can’t be present for the moves, and they supply general strategy and the ability to learn. The limits of rules then drive the story forward. In an unpredictable world the best policy may be the capacity to simulate, to try out actions in the head. Dawkins wonders whether consciousness arises when a brain’s model of the world becomes complete enough to include a model of itself. He offers it as a speculation and doesn’t claim to have solved anything.

Example
Honeybees that remove diseased larvae. W. C. Rothenbuhler’s crosses suggested one gene for uncapping the cell and another for throwing the larva out. Some hybrids did only half of the job.
Since 1976
Later work on hygienic bees found several genetic regions involved, so the neat two-gene story is too simple. The general lesson, that genes influence components of behaviour, has held.
A common misreading
Misreading
“If it’s genetic, it’s fixed.” A program may say learn what tastes sweet or copy your elders. Genes set the rules by which experience shapes the animal.

Conflict

Why the good of the species explains so little, and what takes its place.

The case against group selection

Chapters 1 and 7

The argument that traits rarely evolve “for the good of the species,” because selfish individuals inside a self-sacrificing group outbreed the rest.

The error Dawkins attacks was common in textbooks and natural history films. It ran: predators spare prey, animals fight with restraint, lemmings limit their numbers, because otherwise the species would die out. Williams had pointed out that a species can be destroyed by what is good for its members, and nothing in natural selection looks ahead to prevent this. The decisive consideration is speed. Groups are born and die slowly, while individuals within them turn over quickly, so any advantage groups enjoy is usually undermined from within before it can count. Dawkins presents the theory as one that could work in principle but faces heavy odds in practice. The emphasis matters, because his critics often describe him as having ruled it out on logic alone.

Example
In a group whose members all restrain their breeding, one that doesn’t leaves more offspring. Restraint disappears long before the group’s virtue can save it.
Since 1976
David Sloan Wilson and Elliott Sober revived a modified “multilevel selection”. In 2010 Nowak, Tarnita and E. O. Wilson attacked kin selection theory in Nature; 137 biologists signed a reply defending it. Many theorists consider modern multilevel and inclusive-fitness accounts mathematically equivalent in a wide range of cases, though not everyone agrees.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Groups never matter.” Groups matter greatly as environments. The claim is about which entities selection tends to adapt.
9

The evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS)

Chapter 5

A strategy that, once most of a population adopts it, can’t be bettered by any rare alternative.

The word strategy carries no idea of thinking. It is a pre-programmed policy, such as “attack; if he flees, pursue; if he retaliates, run.” The concept changes the question one asks of behaviour. Before, one asked which behaviour is optimal. But the best thing to do often depends on what everyone else does, and everyone else is adjusting too. Maynard Smith’s idea, with George Price, was to look for the policy that is safe against desertion. Dawkins makes strong claims for it: that it would be seen as one of the most important advances in evolutionary theory since Darwin. It also gives a clear reply to group selectionists. Harmony and restraint can appear in nature because they are stable, and no one has to be aiming at harmony.

Example
Driving on the left is stable where everybody does it. So is driving on the right elsewhere. Neither is better.
The numbers
Maynard Smith’s test for strategy A to resist B: A must do better against A than B does against A; or, if they tie, A must do better against B than B does against B.
Since 1976
Evolutionary game theory became a major field, used in economics, computing and microbiology. Extensions follow the dynamics: populations may cycle forever and never settle.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Stable means best.” A stable state can be worse for everyone than some other arrangement that can’t hold together.
10

Hawks and doves

Chapter 5

The simplest model of fighting. Hawks always escalate. Doves threaten and retreat if attacked.

Two hawks fight until one is badly hurt: on average (50 − 100)/2 = −25 each. Two doves waste time posturing: one gets 50 − 10, the other −10, average 15. A hawk meeting a dove takes 50 and the dove leaves with 0. In a world of doves a single hawk prospers, so hawkishness spreads. In a world of hawks a dove does better (0 beats −25), so dovishness spreads. Neither is stable alone; the mixture is. Notice what the stable mixture costs: 6¼ a contest where universal peace would yield 15. A pact to be doves would benefit everybody, and it would break down because treachery inside the pact pays. Humans can make such pacts work through foresight and enforcement, though, Dawkins adds, only just. This is the book’s first demonstration that what is good for all need not be what evolves.

The numbers
With Dawkins’s points (win 50, lose 0, serious injury −100, long display −10), the stable mixture is 7/12 hawks and 5/12 doves, and the average payoff is 6¼. If all agreed to be doves, everyone would average 15.
Since 1976
Real contests usually involve signals, size and ownership. The model survives as a starting point and is taught everywhere.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Nature must contain hawkish and dovish individuals.” The same balance holds if each animal plays hawk seven times in twelve, at random.

11

Asymmetric contests

Chapter 5

Most fights aren’t between equals. An arbitrary difference, such as who arrived first, can settle a contest without a fight.

Suppose every animal follows the rule “if resident, attack; if intruder, retreat”. An individual that departs from the rule gets into costly fights, so the rule is self-enforcing. The striking point is that the opposite rule, “if resident, retreat; if intruder, attack”, would be equally stable if it got established. The asymmetry needs only to be visible to both. Dawkins calls the reversed case paradoxical. He later reported a candidate in a social spider whose residents move out when an intruder enters. The same logic explains dominance hierarchies: animals that remember the result of the last fight save both sides the cost of repeating it. Peaceful order can be a byproduct of individuals avoiding unprofitable fights.

Example
Speckled wood butterflies contest patches of sunlight. In N. B. Davies’s study the resident always won; when each of two males was tricked into being the resident, they fought for much longer.
Since 1976
Other studies of the same butterfly suggest that physical factors such as body temperature also matter. The “bourgeois” convention remains a standard idea.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Owners win because they are stronger.” They may, but the model shows the convention can be stable even when owners aren’t stronger at all.

Kin selection and Hamilton’s rule

Chapter 6

A gene for helping relatives can spread if relatedness times benefit exceeds the cost: rB > C.

J. B. S. Haldane is said to have joked that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins. Hamilton turned the joke into mathematics. The reasoning uses probability: if I carry a rare gene, what is the chance that my sister has it too? Half. So a gene that makes me save more than two siblings, at the price of my life, saves more than one copy of itself on average for each copy lost. Dawkins adds two refinements. One is life expectancy: a grandparent and grandchild are equally related, but the young one has more breeding ahead, so help flows mostly downwards. The other is certainty. Animals don’t compute genealogies. They follow rules such as “feed whatever squawks in my nest”, which worked on average in the past and which a cuckoo can exploit. A mother’s certainty also exceeds a father’s, which may tell us something about uncles and grandmothers.

Example
A parent feeding its young is the most familiar case. Sterile workers in social insects are the most extreme.
The numbers
Relatedness is 1/2 to parents, children and full siblings; 1/4 to half-siblings, uncles, aunts, nephews, nieces and grandparents; 1/8 to first cousins; 1/32 to second cousins; 1/128 to third cousins; 1 to an identical twin.
Since 1976
Dawkins wrote “Twelve Misunderstandings of Kin Selection” (1979) to deal with such errors. Hamilton’s rule has been tested in many species and extended to microbes.
A common misreading
Misreading
“All humans share over 99% of their DNA, so we should be altruistic to everybody.” What counts is the chance of sharing a particular gene over and above the average in the population.

13

The green beard, and why relatives

Chapter 6

A thought experiment about a gene that gives its bearers a label (a green beard) and makes them kind to anyone with the same label. It shows that the real target of altruism is shared genes, with kinship as the usual clue.

The idea is Hamilton’s, and Dawkins gave it the green beard, to separate two things that usually go together. Suppose one could tell directly whether another carried the gene for altruism; then kinship would be beside the point. In practice a single gene is unlikely to produce a beard, the power to see it and the impulse to help. It is also open to fraud, by mutants that grow the beard and keep the kindness. Kinship is a good clue because it works for every gene at once: any gene I carry has the same chance of being in my sister, so all of them agree on how to treat her. That is why family feeling is widespread and green beards are rare. Dawkins discusses another clue, the armpit effect, in The Extended Phenotype: an animal that takes its own smell as a standard could favour those who smell alike.

Example
In the abstract, any gene that produced the label, the ability to recognize it and the favourable treatment together.
Since 1976
Once regarded as too contrived to exist. Candidate green-beard genes have since been described in fire ants (Gp-9), yeast (FLO1) and slime moulds (csaA).
A common misreading
Misreading
“Altruism is aimed at overall genetic similarity.” A gene is concerned only with copies of itself. Other genes in the body would suppress favouritism that didn’t benefit them.
14

Family planning

Chapter 7

Animals limit their families because too many young would starve. The number that evolves maximizes surviving offspring, not births.

Dawkins separates child-bearing decisions from child-caring decisions. Kin selection helps with the second. The first is a matter of economics. Each extra egg divides the food that parents can bring. Below some number, every egg adds a survivor; above it, each extra lowers everyone’s chance by more than it adds. Restraint is therefore selfish. Wynne-Edwards saw winter flocks and dusk roosts as censuses, gatherings in which the group counted itself and adjusted its breeding. Dawkins suggests that if crowding really does foretell famine, each individual will lay fewer eggs for its own sake. He adds John Krebs’s Beau Geste effect: a bird with a varied song may be pretending to be many birds, to discourage newcomers. The chapter ends with human welfare states, the one place where the link between family size and survival has been cut.

Example
Swifts typically lay two or three eggs. Parents given an extra egg in experiments tend to raise fewer chicks than normal.
Since 1976
Many birds turn out to lay slightly fewer eggs than this year’s optimum, because large families also cost the parents future breeding. Lack’s principle was refined rather than overturned.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Territories and flocking prove animals count themselves for the common good,” which was Wynne-Edwards’s view. Dawkins gives individual explanations for the same facts.

Parent–offspring conflict

Chapter 8

A child values itself twice as much as it values a full sibling. A parent values them equally. So they disagree about how long care should last.

The theory is Robert Trivers’s. Its force lies in a simple fact: the parent is related equally to all its children (1/2), while each child is related to itself by 1 and to a full sibling by 1/2. Nobody needs to be wicked. Each acts as its genes have been selected to act, and a conflict results. Dawkins explores the tactics. A child has private information, since it knows how hungry it is, so it can exaggerate, and parents must allow for that. A fledgling that screams attracts predators, and so holds its parents to a form of blackmail. Cuckoos show how far the devices can go, because they have no sibling interest to restrain them. Dawkins describes a nestling swallow, put experimentally in a magpie’s nest, that threw out an egg as a cuckoo would. He offers no winner. The outcome will be a compromise, and the theory predicts where the quarrel lies, not how it ends. He adds a moral: if we want children to be generous, we must teach it, because we can’t expect it from their biology.

Example
The tantrums of weaning. Chicks that cheep louder than their hunger warrants, risking predators.
The numbers
Feeding the present child helps it (B) and costs future siblings (C). The parent should stop when C exceeds B. The child wants to go on until C exceeds 2B. Between those points lies conflict.
Since 1976
Trivers’s theory was extended by David Haig to pregnancy itself and to genomic imprinting, where genes from father and mother differ in how much they demand from the mother.
A common misreading
Misreading
“The child should always win,” or “the parent must.” R. D. Alexander argued that parents must win, because a child that cheats will suffer from similar cheating by its own young. Dawkins shows the argument can be run backwards with equal force.

The battle of the sexes

Chapter 9

The sexes are defined by the size of their sex cells. Large eggs carry food and are costly; small sperm are cheap. Dawkins derives the exploitation of one parent by the other from that difference.

Suppose all sex cells were once the same size. Slightly larger ones gave their offspring a better start; that opened a way for smaller, faster ones to seek out the large and exploit their provisions. The middle vanished. Because each sperm is cheap, a male can, in principle, have far more offspring than a female, and has more to gain from desertion. What can a female do? Dawkins considers two counter-strategies. In domestic bliss she insists on a long courtship or a nest, so that by the time of mating the male has invested and any rival female would demand the same. In the he-man strategy she gives up hope of help and chooses the best genes. He warns against one mistake along the way, the Concorde fallacy, named after the aircraft: past spending is no reason to continue, and what counts is the future return. His simple payoff model was among the first of its kind.

Example
A model with coy and fast females, faithful and philandering males.
The numbers
With a surviving child worth +15 to each parent, upbringing costing 20 and a long courtship costing 3, the equilibrium is 5/6 coy females and 5/8 faithful males.
Since 1976
Dawkins wrote that the mixture would be stable. Peter Schuster and Karl Sigmund showed in 1981 that it isn’t: the population circles the equilibrium forever. He acknowledges this in the 1989 notes. Feminist biologists, and later work on female competition and sex-role reversal, challenged the assumption that eggs alone set the roles.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Biology says women should be coy.” The model concerns no particular species, and Dawkins points out that human behaviour is heavily shaped by culture.

Experiment 8

Round and round

Dawkins’s payoffs for coy and fast females, faithful and philandering males. He first wrote that the mixture is stable. Release a population and watch.

The cross marks the equilibrium (5/6 coy, 5/8 faithful). The population is neither drawn to it nor driven off. It circles, as Schuster and Sigmund showed in 1981. More coyness makes faithfulness pay; more faithfulness makes speed pay; and so on.

17

Sexual selection and the handicap

Chapter 9

Traits such as a peacock’s tail evolve because they attract mates. Zahavi proposed that they work because they are costly: only a sound animal can afford them.

R. A. Fisher had shown how a fashion can run away with itself. If females prefer slightly longer tails, then long-tailed males have more mates, and females who choose them have sons with the fashionable tail. The preference and the tail increase together, until survival calls a halt. The tail needs to mean nothing. Zahavi’s alternative gives it meaning: a male who has survived with that burden must be good. Dawkins’s first objection was that the offspring would inherit the handicap as well as the quality. The rescue came when theorists supposed that animals vary the size of the handicap to suit their condition, so that a display is more expensive for a weak male than for a strong one. The same chapter covers Fisher’s account of the sex ratio: whichever sex is rarer is the better investment, so equal spending on sons and daughters is stable.

Example
Long tails in birds of paradise. The stotting of gazelles, which leap in front of a predator, as if to say: look how fit I am, chase someone else.
Since 1976
Dawkins wrote in 1976 that he didn’t believe the theory. In 1989 he reversed his position after Alan Grafen’s mathematical models showed that it could work. Not all theorists agree that honest signals must be costly at equilibrium.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Handicaps are good for their bearers.” They reduce survival. They pay only through mating or through being believed.
18

The social insects

Chapter 10

Why workers give up breeding. In ants, bees and wasps, males come from unfertilized eggs, which makes full sisters more closely related to each other than a mother is to her daughters.

A honeybee queen mates early and stores sperm. Fertilized eggs become females with two sets of chromosomes; unfertilized ones become males with a single set. All of a male’s sperm are therefore identical, so sisters with the same father share all of his genes and half of their mother’s, 3/4 in total. From the worker’s point of view, the mother is an efficient machine for making sisters, and it may pay to farm her. Dawkins reports Trivers and Hare’s test, which weighed the sexes in some twenty ant species and found investment near 3:1 in favour of females, as if workers controlled it. In slave-making ants, where the brood is raised by members of another species with no interest in the matter, the ratio was nearer 1:1, as a queen would wish. Later critics questioned parts of the analysis. It remains a striking case of a prediction about conflict within what looks like a perfect society.

The numbers
Full sisters 3/4; mother and daughter 1/2; sister and brother 1/4. Trivers and Hare predicted that workers should prefer to invest in sisters over brothers by 3 to 1.
Since 1976
Termites and naked mole-rats have sterile workers without haplodiploidy. The current emphasis is on strict monogamy in the ancestors of social insects (the work of Jacobus Boomsma, and of William Hughes and colleagues in 2008) and on ecology. If a queen mates with several males, the average relatedness among her daughters falls toward 1/4.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Haplodiploidy explains insect societies.” The 3/4 to sisters is balanced by 1/4 to brothers, an average of 1/2. Something else must tip the scales.

The selfish herd and the alarm call

Chapter 10

Apparent acts of public spirit, such as herding and warning calls, can serve the individual that performs them.

Hamilton imagined each animal surrounded by a domain of danger, the ground nearer to it than to any other. A predator that strikes at random takes whichever animal is nearest. Those on the edge have large domains and try to shrink them by moving inwards, and the result is a packed crowd. Dawkins offers two theories of why a bird gives a thin whistle at the sight of a hawk. In the cave theory (Latin: beware) a lone bird freezing in the grass is still in danger while its companions go on moving and making noise, so it pays to hush them. In the never break ranks theory the bird should fly up to a tree, but not alone, so it calls the others to go too. He considers stotting next, and Zahavi’s reading of it as a message to the predator. The lesson lies in the method: before granting an animal public spirit, ask what else the act might do.

Example
W. D. Hamilton’s herd. Each animal moves to put another between itself and danger, and a tight bunch forms with no one wishing to form it.
Since 1976
Paul Sherman’s study of ground squirrels (1977) found that females with close kin nearby called most, which supported kin selection. FitzGibbon and Fanshawe (1988) showed that stotting gazelles are chased less by wild dogs.
A common misreading
Misreading
“All alarm calls are disguised selfishness.” Some are directed at relatives. The lesson is that several explanations must be tried.

20

Reciprocal altruism

Chapter 10

Helping a stranger can pay if the favour is returned later. It requires repeated meetings, recognition and memory.

Dawkins tells the story with three strategies. Suckers groom anyone. Cheats accept grooming and give none. Grudgers groom strangers, but remember and refuse those who failed to repay. In a population of suckers, cheats sweep through and everyone, including the cheats, ends up dying of ticks. Grudgers are different. If rare, they waste effort on cheats they meet for the first time. If common enough, they mostly meet one another, and they prosper while cheats run out of victims. Both all-cheat and all-grudger populations are stable; the question is which one a population falls into. Dawkins describes a simulation in which the suckers go first, the cheats nearly take over, and grudgers recover slowly from the brink. He ends with human beings, whose long memories may make such games the source of gratitude, guilt, sympathy and money. Trivers suggested as much, and Dawkins presents it as suggestion.

Example
Birds that groom one another’s heads for dangerous ticks. Cleaner fish and their large clients. Vampire bats that share blood.
Since 1976
Gerald Wilkinson’s bats became the classic case. Gerald Carter and Wilkinson (2013) found that past help received predicted sharing better than kinship did. Convincing examples in animals other than humans remain fairly rare.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Reciprocity spreads by itself.” A lone grudger among cheats is helpless. It has to reach a critical number first.

21

The Prisoner’s Dilemma and Tit for Tat

Chapter 12

In a single encounter, betrayal pays whatever the other does. In a repeated one, the most successful known programs were nice, forgiving and not envious.

The added chapter’s title turns the common saying around. Axelrod invited experts to submit programs. Anatol Rapoport’s Tit for Tat cooperates first and then copies the other’s last move. It won. Axelrod published the results, invited new entries, received sixty-two, and it won again. He looked for common features. Nice strategies are never the first to defect; in the first round the top eight were all nice. Forgiving ones don’t dwell on the past. To be not envious is to care about your own score and not about beating your neighbour. The last point leads to what Dawkins thinks the deepest distinction: zero-sum games, in which your gain is my loss, and nonzero-sum games with a banker to be beaten together. Many human quarrels, divorce among them, are nonzero-sum games that the participants, or their lawyers, treat as zero-sum. One more condition is needed: the shadow of the future must be long. In the trenches the same units met daily, and it was when they were moved about that the truce broke down.

Example
Trench warfare in the First World War, where facing units settled into “live and let live”.
The numbers
Axelrod’s usual points: mutual cooperation 3 each; mutual betrayal 1 each; betraying a cooperator 5, and being betrayed 0. In his first tournament fifteen programs (one random) played 200 rounds with each other and themselves. Tit for Tat, with two instructions, averaged 504 of a benchmark 600.
Since 1976
If mistakes occur, two Tit for Tat players can fall into long feuds. Nowak and Sigmund found that generous versions, and a rule called win–stay, lose–shift, can do better. In 2012 Press and Dyson described strategies able to extort their opponents, though these fare poorly as they become common.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Tit for Tat always wins.” It never beats its opponent in a single game. It wins on total score, by getting others to cooperate, and its success depends on who else is playing.

22

Memes

Chapter 11

Units of cultural transmission: tunes, ideas, catch-phrases, fashions, ways of making pots. They leap from brain to brain by imitation.

The purpose was modest. Dawkins wanted to show that replicator is the general notion and DNA one example of it. If there were life elsewhere it would probably evolve by the differential survival of replicating entities too, whatever they were made of. Then he adds that we needn’t go to other worlds, since a new replicator has appeared on this one. He coined meme from the Greek mimeme, shortened to rhyme with cream. What he draws out of it is a warning against asking only what culture does for our genes. A cultural trait may have evolved in the way it has because that is advantageous to itself. Celibacy can’t be inherited by genes, yet the idea can spread. He sees serious difficulties: the fidelity of copying is poor and the boundaries of a unit are unclear. Darwin, he adds, left much of Mendel to be discovered. Our genes are diluted by half in each generation, so that after a few centuries little is left of any individual. Our memes may last better. Socrates may or may not have a gene alive today; his ideas are still going strong.

Example
A chain letter, the opening of a Beethoven symphony, the idea of God, Auld Lang Syne and its misremembered words.
Since 1976
Daniel Dennett and Susan Blackmore developed the idea; an academic journal of memetics ran from 1997 to 2005. The science of cultural evolution grew up mostly on other lines, with Robert Boyd, Peter Richerson and Joseph Henrich. Dan Sperber objected that ideas are reconstructed by each mind rather than copied.
A common misreading
Misreading
“A meme is a funny picture online.” Dawkins has remarked on this second life with amusement: the term mutated, as a replicator might.

Experiment 13

One star is redrawn from the last copy, errors and all. The other is redrawn each time from the rule “five points, two radii”. Set how shaky the hand is.

Dawkins made this argument later, in his foreword to Susan Blackmore’s The Meme Machine (1999), to answer the charge that memes are copied too badly to count as replicators.

23

The rebellion

Chapters 1 and 11

Dawkins’s claim that conscious foresight lets human beings act against the interests of their replicators.

The last sentence of the original book says that we, alone on earth, can rebel against the tyranny of the selfish replicators. It is the sentence most quoted by those who think Dawkins is contradicting himself. His defence is that a brain which models the future can pursue long-term interests instead of immediate ones, and even pursue disinterested goals that have no place in nature. A conspiracy of doves, unstable among animals, is available to beings who can see why it would be best and enforce it. He gives contraception as the everyday proof. A desire put there by genes is satisfied while the purpose it once served is blocked. The rebellion doesn’t need a soul. It needs only that rules installed for one reason can be used for another. The deeper worry, about who the “we” is, he leaves unresolved, and it is fair to call it the book’s largest loose end.

Example
Contraception. Adoption. Anything done from principle that costs the doer offspring.
Since 1976
Philosophers point to a gap. If brains are built by genes and stocked by memes, who is rebelling? Susan Blackmore accepted the problem and denied that there is an independent self. Others reply that a capacity can exceed the purpose for which it evolved.
A common misreading
Misreading
“If genes are selfish, altruism is impossible or unnatural.” The book draws the opposite moral: teach generosity because you can’t rely on nature to supply it.

The long reach

Where a gene’s influence ends, and why there are bodies at all.

24

The extended phenotype

Chapter 13

A gene’s effects don’t stop at the skin. Houses, dams and even the behaviour of other animals can be parts of its phenotype.

The steps are gradual on purpose. Geneticists are content to say that a gene affects eye colour, though its direct effect is on a protein and everything after that is a long chain. If so, why stop at the body wall? A caddis larva builds a house from stones; choice of stones varies, and it may be partly inherited; so there are genes for stone shape, in just the sense in which there are genes for eye colour. Beaver lakes follow. Next come parasites. A snail with a fluke makes a thicker shell than is good for the snail, and Dawkins argues that we may treat the extra thickness as an adaptation of fluke genes. Finally there’s action at a distance, as when a young cuckoo’s gape works on a foster parent like a drug. He sums up with what he calls the central theorem: an animal’s behaviour tends to maximize the survival of the genes for that behaviour, whether or not those genes happen to be in the body of the animal performing it.

Example
The stone house of a caddis larva. Beaver dams. Flukes whose snail hosts grow thicker shells.
Since 1976
Dawkins regards it as his main contribution. A fungus that makes ants climb and bite before they die, and a virus linked to caterpillars’ climbing, are often discussed as examples; in the virus, particular genes have been identified.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Anything an organism affects is its extended phenotype.” Only effects that influence the survival of the genes responsible count. An architect’s building is no more likely to survive or fail because of variations in the architect’s genes.

25

Shared exits

Chapters 10 and 13

Whether a parasite harms or helps depends on how its genes leave the body. If they leave in the host’s eggs, the two have the same interests.

Two parasites live in the same host. One passes to the host’s young inside the eggs. The other spreads in the host’s breath or bites. The first gains by everything that makes its host survive, court and breed; over time it may become helpful, then indispensable, then indistinguishable from a part. The second cares only that the host lives long enough to spread it, and will exploit the host to that end. Now turn the argument round. Why do our own genes cooperate? For the same reason: they all have the same way out, through sperm or egg. If a gene found some other exit, it would treat the body as a parasite does. Segregation distorters, which cheat in the making of sperm, show that this isn’t idle. So the harmony of the body has a cause that could have failed to hold. It rests on one narrow gate through which all the genes must pass together.

Example
Mitochondria, descended from free-living bacteria, travel only in the egg, and have become a part of us.
Since 1976
The principle is a standard part of research on virulence. Wolbachia bacteria, passed through eggs but not through sperm, illustrate it with sinister exactness: in several species they kill, feminize or make redundant the males that can’t transmit them.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Parasites always evolve to be harmless.” Mildness follows only if transmission depends on the host’s own survival and reproduction.
26

The bottleneck and the organism

Chapter 13

Why life comes in large bodies that begin as single cells. Passing through one cell gives each generation a fresh start, a regular cycle and a uniform set of genes.

Three advantages are given. Back to the drawing board: an adult organ can’t easily be remodelled into a radically different one, whereas a fresh start from one cell allows changes early in development, as swords are turned into ploughshares by melting them down and not by hammering. Orderly timing: a fixed cycle gives genes a calendar, so that they can be switched on at set stages. Uniformity: all of the cells in bottle-wrack descend from one spore and so share their genes; a group of chunks becomes a patchwork of mutants, with cells that have differing interests. So bottlenecked life is where real cooperation, and therefore complicated bodies, can evolve. The question Dawkins asks here is the one few biologists had thought to put: why did genes come together in cells, cells in bodies, and bodies in a life cycle with a bottleneck? On this view the organism is a result to be accounted for, and evolution could have proceeded without it.

Example
Two invented seaweeds. Splurge-weed breaks off in chunks; bottle-wrack returns every time to a single-celled spore.
Since 1976
The evolution of individuality, or the “major transitions”, became a field in its own right after John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry’s 1995 book. Experiments with yeast and algae have since produced simple multicellularity in the laboratory, sometimes with life cycles that pass through single cells.
A common misreading
Misreading
“Organisms are obvious units.” Dawkins thinks we should be surprised that the replicators have gathered into such well-defined bundles.

Themes and takeaways

What stays with you.

Follow what lasts

The long-lived thing in evolution is information, copied through short-lived bodies.

Levels can disagree

Selfishness at one level can produce altruism at another.

Stable isn’t good

What evolves is what can’t be undermined. It may leave everyone worse off.

Harmony needs explaining

In families, societies and even bodies, cooperation is a result and not a given.

Eight to carry away

  1. Ask who benefits, and by what route, before calling an act generous.
  2. Separate what is copied from what is used.
  3. The best policy depends on what others do. Find what is stable, then ask if it is any good.
  4. Past spending is no reason to go on. (Wasps and ministries both forget.)
  5. In repeated dealings be nice, prompt to answer, quick to forgive and free of envy.
  6. To get cooperation, lengthen the shadow of the future.
  7. An idea may spread because it is good at spreading.
  8. Generosity has to be taught.

The title

The emphasis belongs on the second word. The question is which entity is selfish, and the answer, “the gene”, is why individuals need not be. Dawkins later thought The Immortal Gene would have been safer, and that The Cooperative Gene would have been just as true.

Recurring images

  • The pack of cardsGenes are shuffled and survive the hands they make.
  • The rowing crewTeams arise when individuals are chosen in the company of others.
  • The gangsterIf someone has long prospered in a hard world, you can guess at their qualities. So with genes.
  • The Necker cubeOne set of facts and two ways to see it, gene or organism.
  • The cuckooA rule that used to be safe, and something that has learned to exploit it.

Style and craft

A puzzle, the tempting wrong answer, its removal, and then the right one from definitions.

The prose is plain and quick. Dawkins moves by analogy, says when an analogy is about to break, and translates back into what he calls respectable language. He asks in the preface that the book be read almost as though it were science fiction.

Agents with names

Hawks, suckers and coy females are strategies in costume. They make mathematics visible. They also invite readers to think that genes scheme.

Two voices

The endnotes of 1989 comment on the young author, sometimes amused, sometimes retracting. Few popular books argue with themselves so well.

The close of Chapter 2

The long history of the replicators arrives, in its last lines, at the reader’s own body. He meant the stomach to drop.

The cost

“Lumbering robots” was quoted against him for decades. Critics, he complained, had read the book by title only.

Philosophy

Reductionist in method and humanist in hope.

Complex things are to be explained by simpler ones, a step at a time. Nature has no purposes, though it produces things that look purposive. Values must come from us. Three questions run beneath the book and are not all answered in it.

Where it strains

  • Cause or ledgerAre genes the agents of selection, or the book in which results are recorded? Gould said the second. Dawkins: differences in genes make differences to survival, reliably, and that is cause enough.
  • The elastic geneA unit defined as whatever lasts long enough can’t be refuted. It must be judged by its use.
  • Who rebels?If genes built the brain and memes stocked it, who is the “we” that can defy both? The largest loose end in the book.
  • The metaphor“Selfish” is defined technically and then enjoyed for its ordinary drama.
The deeper claim

A blind statistical process can account for the look of purpose, including love, cheating and war, if we have correctly identified what persists. Knowing where our dispositions come from is the first step toward not being ruled by them.

Criticism and counterpoints

Turn each card for the reply.

The case against, at full strength

The book takes one useful perspective and presents it as the truth of life. Once “gene” means whatever persists, “genes are the unit of selection” is nearly a tautology, and the causal work is done by organisms, relations and environments kept offstage. Its vocabulary of selfishness, robots and tyranny makes a bleak picture that the mathematics doesn’t require, and generations have come away thinking science has shown people to be selfish at bottom.

The fair reply

Perspectives are how science moves, and this one solved problems others couldn’t: sterile workers, weaning quarrels, cheating chromosomes. Its definitions are on the first pages, and its closing message is the opposite of the one complained of.

What has dated

The two-gene bee, the stable mixture of coy and fast, the first view of handicaps (reversed in 1989), and some language about the sexes. Nothing in the 1976 text foresees the scale of regulatory DNA, horizontal gene transfer or the microbiome.

How to read it

In a 1989 or later edition, with a finger in the endnotes.

If you read nothing else

Chapter 2 for the idea as a story of origins. Chapter 5 for stability in place of the good of the species. Chapter 12, the most hopeful and useful. Chapter 13, the most original.

Take 1–4 slowly, since the definitions are there. Do 5, 6 and 10 with a pencil.
