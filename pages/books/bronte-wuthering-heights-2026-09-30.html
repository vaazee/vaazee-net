In brief

A revenge tragedy dressed as a love story, told secondhand by two unreliable narrators about two houses, two families, and two generations on the Yorkshire moors. Its real subject is not romance but identity: what happens when two people believe they are the same being, and a world of property, class, and marriage pulls them apart.

Emily Brontë's only novel was received as monstrous in 1847. It is now one of the few English novels that seems to belong to no school at all. Read it for the frame, the weather, and the second half that most adaptations leave out.

"Whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same."— Catherine Earnshaw, chapter 9

1Identity & Context

A pseudonymous first novel, rushed into print on the coat-tails of Jane Eyre.

  • TitleWuthering Heights. "Wuthering," Lockwood tells us, is "a significant provincial adjective, descriptive of the atmospheric tumult to which its station is exposed in stormy weather."
  • AuthorEmily Jane Brontë, writing as Ellis Bell
  • PublishedDecember 1847, Thomas Cautley Newby, London. It filled volumes I–II of a three-volume set, with Anne's Agnes Grey as volume III. Charlotte published a corrected edition in 1850.
  • FormA frame narrative that mixes Gothic romance, domestic realism, revenge tragedy, and regional pastoral
  • Written / setWritten 1845–46, during the "Hungry Forties" and the start of the Irish famine. Set 1771–1802: the age of Romanticism, revolution, and enclosure.
  • Length34 chapters, about 107,000 words. Two houses four miles apart, three decades, one revenge.

Occasion. In 1845 Charlotte found Emily's poems and pushed the three sisters into print. Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell (1846) sold two copies. Each sister then wrote a novel. Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey were accepted, but Jane Eyre reached print first, in October 1847, and became a sensation. Newby then rushed the other two "Bell" novels out to profit from the name.

2The Author — Brief Note

"Stronger than a man, simpler than a child." — Charlotte on Emily

Emily Jane Brontë (1818–1848) was the fifth of six children of Patrick Brontë, an Irish-born Anglican curate, and grew up in the parsonage at Haworth on the edge of the West Yorkshire moors. Her mother died when she was three. Her two eldest sisters died after time at the Cowan Bridge school, which Charlotte later portrayed as Lowood. Emily left home only for short, unhappy periods: Roe Head, a teaching post at Law Hill near Halifax (1838–39), and Brussels with Charlotte (1842). She died of tuberculosis at thirty, three months after her brother Branwell and about a year after the novel appeared. She is said to have refused a doctor until her last day.

Preoccupations. From childhood she and Anne co-wrote Gondal, an imaginary island kingdom of usurpers, prisoners, doomed lovers, and exiles. Only her poems from it survive. Those poems ("No coward soul is mine", "Remembrance", "The Prisoner") return again and again to a self that refuses consolation, a freedom found only beyond the body, love stronger than death, and nature as an absolute, impersonal force.

Threads into the bookBranwell's collapse into drink, opium, and debt (1845–48) seems close to Hindley's ruin. The Law Hill district had a local legend of an adopted boy who took over a family's property, and High Sunderland Hall, with its grotesque carved front, sits nearby. Her father's Irish roots and her reading of German Gothic (Hoffmann) also show through. The ruined farmhouse at Top Withens above Haworth is popularly called "the Heights", though no real building matches the description.

Reputation. In the 19th century Emily was overshadowed by Charlotte, and Charlotte's 1850 preface helped make her a myth: an untaught genius who did not fully know what she had made. Modern criticism rejects that view and treats her as a deliberate, highly controlled artist. Many readers now rank her above Charlotte.

Among the sisters' novels it is an outlier. Jane Eyre and Agnes Grey are first-person stories of a governess growing into herself. Wuthering Heights has no central consciousness, no moral education, and no narrator the author endorses. Its real relatives are Emily's own poems.

4Plot & Structure

Two houses, two generations, and a story that begins near its end.

Setting. Wuthering Heights is an old, exposed farmhouse belonging to the Earnshaws. Thrushcross Grange, four miles away in a sheltered park, belongs to the genteel Lintons.

Who's who — the two families

First generation (1771–1784): Heathcliff comes between the Earnshaw siblings, loses Catherine to Edgar Linton, and marries Edgar's sister Isabella out of spite.

  • 4The foundling. 1771 — Nelly Dean takes over the story. Old Mr Earnshaw comes home from Liverpool with a dark, ragged child and names him Heathcliff, after a son who had died. Catherine adores him; Hindley hates him.
  • 5–7Degradation and the dog-bite. 1777 — Hindley inherits and makes Heathcliff a labourer. Spying on the Lintons, Catherine is bitten by their dog and stays five weeks at the Grange. She comes home a lady.
  • 9"I am Heathcliff" key scene 1780 — Catherine accepts Edgar Linton. Heathcliff overhears "it would degrade me to marry Heathcliff" and leaves before she says "he's more myself than I am."
  • 10–14The return. 1783 — Three years later Heathcliff comes back rich and polished. He wins the Heights from Hindley at cards and elopes with Isabella to hurt the Lintons. Isabella's letter (ch. 13) describes the abuse that follows.
  • 15–16Catherine's death key scene 1784 — A last, savage embrace. She dies after giving birth to Cathy. Heathcliff: "I cannot live without my life! I cannot live without my soul!"
  • 17Flight and ruin. 1784 — Isabella escapes south and gives birth to Linton. Hindley dies drunk, mortgaged to Heathcliff, who keeps the boy Hareton as a brute.
  • 18Thirteen years in one chapter. 1797 — Cathy grows up sheltered at the Grange. After Isabella dies, Heathcliff claims his sickly son Linton.
  • 21–26The courtship trap. 1800 — Heathcliff encourages a romance between Cathy and the dying Linton. Married to Linton, Cathy will bring him the Grange.
  • 27–28Imprisonment and forced marriage turn 1801 — Heathcliff locks Cathy and Nelly in the Heights and forces the wedding. Edgar dies, then Linton. Heathcliff now owns both houses.
  • 29–30Caught up. 1801 — Cathy is left a bitter widow at the Heights: the woman Lockwood met in chapter 1. The flashback has reached the present.
  • 31–33A strange change. 1802 — Lockwood returns a year later. Cathy is teaching Hareton to read, and they are falling in love. Heathcliff has lost the will to finish his revenge: "I have lost the faculty of enjoying their destruction, and I am too idle to destroy for nothing."
  • 34The open window ending 1802 — Heathcliff stops eating, seems to see Catherine everywhere, and is found dead in the oak-panelled bed with the window swinging open. He is buried beside her; he had earlier bribed the sexton to remove the facing sides of their coffins. Locals see two ghosts on the moor. Hareton and Cathy will marry and move to the Grange. Lockwood, at the graves, "wondered how any one could ever imagine unquiet slumbers for the sleepers in that quiet earth."

Spoiler-free or full arc?

Spoiler-free: Heathcliff's revenge succeeds. By 1801 he owns both houses, the heirs are in his power, and Lockwood leaves a household frozen in hatred. How does a story that cruel end? Read chapters 31–34 and find out.

Almost half the book covers the second generation, which most adaptations cut.

Nested narration

The legal clockworkIn "The Structure of Wuthering Heights" (1926), C. P. Sanger showed that the dates, ages, and inheritance law (entail, coverture, wills) are worked out almost to the day. Heathcliff gains both estates by entirely legal means. The wildest novel of its era is also one of its most precisely built.

5Characters

Seen from outside, through what they say and do. Their inner lives show as weather.

HHeathclifffoundling, avengerboth houses

Of unknown race and parentage ("a little Lascar, or an American or Spanish castaway"), he has one name, and it was a dead child's. Abused, he becomes an abuser. He is the Byronic hero turned inside out: his cruelty is shown in detail, while his love comes to us almost entirely through others' reports. An outsider who masters the system that shut him out (property, law, marriage) and then finds no meaning in owning it.

CCatherine Earnshaw (Linton)"I am Heathcliff"Heights → Grange

Imperious, wild, self-dramatising, and the book's centre of gravity even though she dies halfway through. She wants to be both Heathcliff's other self and Edgar's wife. Her marriage fixes her identity, and she tries to escape it through fever, starving herself, and death. "I'm tired of being enclosed here."

NNelly (Ellen) Deannarratorservant to both

Often taken as the voice of common sense. Since James Hafley's "The Villain in Wuthering Heights" (1958), critics have counted how often she meddles: hiding letters, delaying warnings, judging harshly. She is the book's most interesting unreliable witness.

LMr Lockwoodframe narratortenant

A vain, conventional southerner who calls himself a misanthrope and is out of his depth. His misreadings in the opening chapters (he takes a heap of dead rabbits for cats) put us in his position as outsiders. His dream of the child at the window is the novel's central image.

EEdgar Lintonthe civilised husbandGrange

Gentle, cultivated, loyal, and slightly weak, seen through the scorn of the wilder characters. Brontë does not mock him. His devotion to Catherine and to his daughter is real.

IIsabella Lintondisillusioned brideGrange → south

She mistakes Heathcliff for a romantic hero and is quickly taught otherwise. Her letter (ch. 13) is one of the most frightening accounts of domestic abuse in Victorian fiction. Her escape is an act of courage.

hHindley Earnshawfirst oppressorHeights

A resentful heir who becomes a drunken tyrant after his wife dies. Heathcliff's first oppressor and first victim.

cCathy Linton (Heathcliff, Earnshaw)her mother, softenedGrange → Heights → Grange

Her mother's daughter, with the edges softened. Her three possible surnames echo the three names scratched on the window-ledge. In the end she rewrites the story her mother could not escape.

HaHareton Earnshawthe brutalised heirHeights

Deliberately degraded, as Heathcliff was, and loyal to his abuser anyway. His rise to literacy and love is the quiet counterweight to Heathcliff's ruin.

LiLinton Heathcliffthe product of hatesouth → Heights

Sickly, whining, and selfish: the stunted child of a marriage made out of spite. Repellent, and also pitiable.

JJosephthe CalvinistHeights

Speaks broad Yorkshire dialect and damns everyone. Comic and grim at once, and he outlives them all.

6Themes & Key Takeaways

Love as identity, revenge as inheritance, and property behind everything.

Major themes

  1. Love as identity, not affection. Catherine and Heathcliff do not want to have each other; they want to be each other. That kind of love is incompatible with marriage, society, and finally with having a body.
  2. Revenge reproduces itself. Heathcliff does to Hareton exactly what Hindley did to him. The cycle breaks only when the avenger loses interest.
  3. Property, class, and law shape every bond. Catherine's marriage, Heathcliff's rise, and the forced wedding are all economic acts. The romance happens inside a fight over property titles.
  4. Nature versus culture. The Heights and the Grange each offer something and each cost something. The ending joins them rather than choosing between them.
  5. The outsider. Heathcliff's unclear race and origin raise questions of empire, Irish migration, and how a community creates the monster it then fears.
  6. Imprisonment and escape. Locked doors, barred windows, and box beds run through the book, and so does the longing to get beyond them.
  7. Reading and writing as power. Diaries in margins, hidden letters, books thrown into the fire, and finally a reading lesson that heals.
Key takeaways
  • This is not a romance to be copied. It is a study of destructive attachment, and the book knows it.
  • Read the narrators, not just the story. Much of the meaning sits in the gap between what Nelly judges and what she reports.
  • The second half matters. The second generation is the novel's argument.
  • Heathcliff's revenge is legal. The cruelty works through ordinary institutions, which is the most modern thing about the book.
  • The ending is ambiguous on purpose. Folk ghosts, Lockwood's "quiet earth", and a marriage offer three different kinds of peace.

The title. The book is named after a house, not a person. The house is buffeted, exposed, and "wuthering", and it holds the whole storm. By the end it has gone back to its rightful heir, and the storm is only weather again.

7Style & Craft

Plain sentences, extreme speech, and no authorial voice at all.

Prose. Clear, hard, and surprisingly plain for so violent a book. The narrative sentences are sober and orderly, while the dialogue is extreme and incantatory. Brontë saves her lyrical writing for set speeches and for nature. Joseph's broad Yorkshire dialect, which Charlotte toned down in 1850, ties the book to an actual place.

Technique. The two layers of narration cool the material, push the passion into the past, and make its truth uncertain. The time scheme is exact and the cast is closed, which gives the book the compression of drama.

Voice. Its only clear signature is the absence of moral commentary. Brontë never steps in to judge, and that alarmed Victorian readers more than anything.

"My love for Linton is like the foliage in the woods: time will change it, I'm well aware, as winter changes the trees. My love for Heathcliff resembles the eternal rocks beneath: a source of little visible delight, but necessary. Nelly, I am Heathcliff!"— ch. 9

Romantic love put in geological terms. The Lintons are seasonal and Heathcliff is bedrock. The comparison is not flattering ("little visible delight") but existential ("necessary").

"I lingered round them, under that benign sky… and wondered how any one could ever imagine unquiet slumbers for the sleepers in that quiet earth."— ch. 34, the last sentence

The final word goes back to the obtuse Lockwood. His calm is either a blessing or a last failure to understand, since we have just heard that the ghosts walk.

8Philosophical & Intellectual Analysis

A world run on energies rather than morals.

Worldview. Nature here is not good, as in Wordsworth, and not ruled by Providence, as Joseph believes. It is a field of powerful, impersonal forces, shown without consolation. Brontë's poems suggest a mystic, almost heretical spirituality: a self that goes beyond its body and finds heaven in the earth. Catherine dreams she went to heaven and wept to come home, until the angels flung her "into the middle of the heath on the top of Wuthering Heights; where I woke sobbing for joy."

  • RomanticismByron's outcast hero and love as transcendence: the novel takes up the fantasy and then counts its cost
  • CalvinismJoseph and the sermon in Lockwood's first dream. Brontë mocks a theology of damnation and also borrows its absolute stakes.
  • German GothicHoffmann, Tieck, the doppelgänger, the uncanny house
  • Property lawEntail and coverture: a married woman's lack of legal standing
  • Later readingsMarxist (Eagleton, Myths of Power, 1975), feminist (Gilbert & Gubar, The Madwoman in the Attic, 1979), Irish/postcolonial (Eagleton, Heathcliff and the Great Hunger, 1995), transgressive (Bataille, Literature and Evil)

Tensions

  • The book makes the lovers' bond its most compelling thing, and also shows that bond causing misery across two generations.
  • It is a radical critique of property and class, yet it ends by giving the property back to the legitimate heir.
  • Its ghosts may be real or may be products of grief. The frame never settles which.

Questions it asks

  • Can a person be completed only by someone else, and if so, what does that do to everyone else?
  • Is cruelty learned or innate? The novel says learned, but shows how hard the learning is to undo.
  • Is peace possible in this life, or only in the grave?
The deeper claimThere are two kinds of love and two kinds of time. One is absolute and timeless, incompatible with society and fulfilled only in death and the earth. The other is social and historical: patient, teachable, possible in the world. The novel honours the first and lets the second inherit the house. We need both stories, and whoever tells them (Nelly, Lockwood, the reader) will always be partly wrong.

9Reception, Significance & Influence

From "inexpressibly painful" to the canon.

  • 1848Baffled reviewers. Douglas Jerrold's: "a strange sort of book — baffling all regular criticism; yet it is impossible to begin and not finish it." The Atlas called it "inexpressibly painful." Five such reviews were found in Emily's writing desk after her death.
  • 1850Charlotte's preface. She defends her sister and also apologises for her: "Whether it is right or advisable to create beings like Heathcliff, I do not know: I scarcely think it is."
  • 1883Swinburne praises it. Rossetti had already called it "a fiend of a book", with admiration.
  • 1916Virginia Woolf sets Emily above Charlotte: "she looked out upon a world cleft into gigantic disorder."
  • 1934Lord David Cecil reads it as a cosmic struggle between storm and calm.
  • 1939Wyler's film (Olivier, Oberon) cuts the second generation and fixes the romantic image for generations of viewers.
  • 1948F. R. Leavis leaves it out of The Great Tradition as "a kind of sport". Q. D. Leavis later gave it a full social reading (1969).
  • 1978Kate Bush, "Wuthering Heights", sung in the voice of Catherine's ghost: a UK No. 1.
  • 1975–95The theory years. Eagleton, Gilbert & Gubar, and Irish and postcolonial readings make it one of the most theorised novels in English.
  • 2011Andrea Arnold's film, the first with a Black Heathcliff. Raw and almost wordless.
  • 2026Emerald Fennell's "Wuthering Heights" (Robbie, Elordi). A stylised, deliberately anachronistic erotic version that divided critics and set off a new wave of reading the novel.

10Criticism & Counterpoints

Great atmosphere, or great novel?

Common critiques

  • Melodrama: too many fevers, curses, and faintings.
  • Hard to like: almost nobody to admire, and selfish, cruel lovers at the centre.
  • Structure: the second half can feel like a smaller, schematic repeat of the first.
  • Dialect: Joseph's Yorkshire slows many readers down.
  • Dated elements: Heathcliff's racial othering is never seen from his side, and the abuse is presented without commentary.
The case againstThe book's fame rests on misreading. It half-encourages a cultural fantasy of obsessive, possessive, abusive love as the highest form of passion, and its narrative brilliance does not cover the thinness of its lovers' inner lives. On this view it is a great atmosphere more than a great novel: a Gothic poem stretched to novel length.
The defenceThe excess is the point, and the structure frames and judges it even when no narrator does. The second generation is a deliberate correction, not a repeat. The novel never asks us to admire Heathcliff, only to understand how he was made and to see what possessive love costs. The romantic misreading comes mostly from the 1939 film, which stopped at Catherine's death.

11Who Should Read It & How

Short, fierce, and with no moral sermon.

Ideal reader. Anyone interested in passion, cruelty, and how a story's form controls its meaning. It also suits readers who think they dislike Victorian novels.

Prerequisites. None. Keep the family tree above open, because the repeated names (Catherine, Linton, Earnshaw) cause confusion.

  • Push through chapters 1–3. Lockwood's confusion is deliberate, and it pays off.
  • Read the narrators sceptically. Watch what Nelly does as well as what she says.
  • Do not stop at Catherine's death (ch. 16). The second half is where the novel makes its argument.
  • Read Joseph aloud. The dialect makes sense once you hear it.

If you read nothing else

Chapters 3, 9 & 15 · about 40 pages

Chapter 3: Lockwood's night in the box bed, the diary in the margins, and the ghost at the window. The frame and the haunting together.

Chapter 9: "I am Heathcliff". The love, and the betrayal that sets everything going.

Chapter 15: the last meeting. The cost.
