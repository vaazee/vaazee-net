In brief

A short, bright, fast-moving Telugu kriti in which Tyagaraja pleads with his own mind: “Won't you listen to my plea? I'll tell you the whole secret.” The secret is that the Lord became human to show how to live: conduct (naḍata) over ritual. The raga, Nalinakanti (“lustre of the lotus”), is practically known through this one song. Listen for the backward step G → R → M that opens every ascent: that zig-zag is the raga.

“kani mānavāvatāruḍai kanipiñcināḍē naḍata”
Seeing this, He became incarnate as a man and showed us how to walk.— Charanam

1Identity & Context

One of the Trinity, talking to his own mind about what religion is actually for.

TitlePopularly Manavyālakiñcarādaṭē; the authoritative texts print manavin(i) ālakiñca rādaṭē (manavini “my plea” + ālakiñca “to listen”). The manavyā- form is a singers' elision that became the common name.
ComposerTyagaraja (1767–1847), Tiruvaiyaru. A vāggēyakāra (writes both words and music); one of the Carnatic Trinity with Muthuswami Dikshitar and Syama Sastri.
Raga · TalaNalinakanti (janya of 27th mela Sarasangi) · Adi (some notations: Desadi)
LanguageTelugu
DateUnknown. Passed down orally through Tyagaraja's disciples and written down only in the 19th–20th centuries.
FamilyOne of many songs addressed to the mind, alongside Manasā etulōrtunē (Malayamarutam) and Manasu nilpa śakti lēkapōtē (Abhogi).

The setting is the Thanjavur Maratha era, when Vedic ritualism and royal patronage dominated. Tyagaraja turned down court service and lived by nāma-siddhānta, devotion through singing the name. In this song he states plainly, almost like a sermon, that ethical living matters more than ritual for its own sake.

2Formal Structure

Pallavi → anupallavi → one charanam: a statement, credentials, an argument, and the plea again.

PPallavi“manavinālakiñca rādaṭē”madhya sthayirefrain

The thesis and refrain. It enters off the beat (the Desadi eduppu), which gives it a lilting pick-up feel. It carries several sangatis, each adding a little more ornament or range. This is the standard spot for kalpanaswaram.

AAnupallavi“ghanuḍaina rāma candruni”toward tara Ṡcredentials

The melody climbs toward the upper Sa. The speaker establishes his authority: I am the one who knows Rama's compassionate heart, so listen to me.

CCharanam“karma kāṇḍa matākṛṣṭulai…”narrativeargument + mudra

More syllables per beat, closer to speech. It tells a short parable: people lured by ritual wander the forest of samsara; the Lord takes human form and demonstrates right conduct. It closes with the signature tyāgarāju and goes back to the pallavi.

3The Raga — Nalinakanti

“Lustre of the lotus”: bright, open and hopeful, with one crooked step that gives it its identity.

Parent 27 Sarasangi (S R₂ G₃ M₁ P D₁ N₃)
Type Shadava–shadava, vakra arohana
Arohana S G₃ R₂ M₁ P N₃ Ṡ
Avarohana Ṡ N₃ P M₁ G₃ R₂ S
Omitted Dhaivatam (Dha)
Mood bright, joyful, persuasive

The crooked ascent — compare with neighbouring ragas

Nalinakanti: S G R M P N Ṡ. It overshoots to Ga, steps back to Ri, then climbs. This “lean back, then leap” is the raga's fingerprint.

4Drone, Not Harmony

All the tension comes from where a phrase sits relative to the S–P drone.

There are no chords. The tambura sounds S–P–Ṡ throughout, and the music lives in how far a phrase strays from those anchors and how it comes back. Two things stand out in Nalinakanti. First, with no Dha, the descent drops N → P directly, so Pa feels like a second resting place. Second, the G–R reversal puts a small “lean back before leaping forward” into every climb.

Music mirrors meaning The song tells the mind to turn inward and look again before pressing on, and the raga's ascent does exactly that. It is an interpretive reading, but a satisfying one.

5Rhythm, Tala & Tempo

An 8-beat Adi cycle with an off-beat entry.

  • Adi tala: 8 beats per avartana = a laghu of 4 (clap + 3 finger counts) + two drutams of 2 (clap–wave, clap–wave).
  • Desadi: the label some notations use. It is the same 8-beat cycle, but the song's eduppu (starting point) comes after the samam (beat 1), so “ma-na-vi” sounds like a pick-up.
  • Kalam: medium to brisk (madhyama kala), which suits its common role early in a concert or as a lively filler after a heavy main piece.
  • When kalpanaswaras end with a korvai, it has to land precisely on that off-beat eduppu. This is where good mridangam players shine.

6Ensemble & Texture

A standard kutcheri setup: voice, violin (shadowing the voice and answering in solo turns), mridangam, optionally ghatam / kanjira, and the tambura drone. The texture is heterophonic: the violin plays the same line a hair behind the singer, with its own ornaments. The kriti's quick, bright phrases also make it a favourite on veena, mandolin, flute and saxophone.

7Lyrics, Meaning & Emotional Arc

Full text with translation, a word-by-word gloss, and the ideas behind it.

Pallavi

manavin(ā)lakiñca rādaṭē
marmam(e)lla telpedanē manasā

O my mind! Won't you listen to my plea? I'll reveal all the secrets to you.

Anupallavi

ghanuḍaina rāma candruni
karuṇ(ā)ntaraṅgamu telisina nā (manavi…)

Won't you listen to the plea of me, one who knows the compassionate heart of great Ramachandra?

Charanam

karma kāṇḍa mat(ā)kṛṣṭulai bhava
gahana cārulai gāsi jeṇḍaga
kani mānav(ā)vatāruḍai
kanipiñcināḍē naḍata — tyāgarāju (manavi…)

Seeing people suffer, wandering in the dense forest of worldly existence, lured by the doctrines of the ritual portion of the Vedas, the Lord took birth as a human being and showed by example how to conduct oneself. So, won't you listen to this Tyagaraja's plea?

Karma kāṇḍa vs jñāna kāṇḍa The Vedas are traditionally split into a ritual section (sacrifices and rites for heaven or prosperity) and a knowledge section (the Upanishads). Tyagaraja echoes Bhagavad Gita 2.42–44, where Krishna criticises those “who delight in the flowery words of the Vedas” and chase heaven and pleasure; their minds never settle.
The “secret” It isn't esoteric at all. God came as a human, Rama the maryādā puruṣōttama, so that the way to liberation would be conduct you can imitate, not ritual you must perform.

Who is talking to whom? The devotee pleads with his own mind as if it were a stubborn friend. A common reading is that the discerning self is coaxing the restless, ritual-loving self. Commentators have also compared it to a dialogue between the conscious and subconscious mind.

What the mapping shows No D appears anywhere: the raga's missing Dha holds through every word. The pallavi's ma-na-vyā-ḷa (M M mg R S) traces the G→R curl in miniature. The two halves of the charanam work differently. karma kāṇḍa… bhava gahana stays low, circling Pa, which suits the slog through the forest of samsara. Then kani mānavāvatāruḍai kanipiñcināḍē naḍata reuses the anupallavi's high melody note for note, so the arrival of God as man is sung to the same music as śrī rāma candruni.

9Significance & Influence

  • A raga known through one song. For most rasikas, Nalinakanti is Manavyalakinchara. It is the standard example of how the raga should be handled.
  • A teaching staple. Short, bright and full of clear sangatis, it is commonly taught to intermediate students.
  • A crossover favourite. The catchy pallavi has drawn many reinterpretations, notably the Carnatic-rock band Agam's “Manavyalakinchara (Mist of Capricorn)”, as well as many instrumental covers.
  • A textual debate. Scholarly editions print manavinālakiñca; nearly every singer says manavyālakiñca. It is a small example of oral tradition reshaping a text.

10Performance Practice & Recordings

  • Alapana or not. As a filler it often starts straight in. As a sub-main piece, a 3–5 minute alapana that avoids Dha and dwells on G-R-M and P-N-Ṡ.
  • Kalpanaswaram almost always at the pallavi line, landing on the off-beat eduppu.
  • Neraval is rarer. A natural spot is kani mānavāvatāruḍai kanipiñcināḍē naḍata, the line that carries the meaning.
ArtistWhy seek it out
Nedunuri Krishnamurthy (vocal)Unhurried and deeply felt; lets the words breathe. Often cited as a model reading.

11Listening Guide

A typical ~8-minute concert rendition. Times are approximate.

  • 0:00Short alapana Alapana — no Dha anywhere; the S G R M zig-zag; long rests on Pa and Ṡ.
  • 1:30Pallavi enters P — the off-beat pick-up. Sangati 1 is plain; each repeat adds a twist.
  • 2:45Anupallavi A — ghanuḍaina rāma candruni climbs to the upper Ṡ; brightness peaks on candruni.
  • 3:45Charanam C — denser syllables, closer to speech: karma kāṇḍa…
  • 4:30“kani mānavāvatāruḍai” meaning peak C — the turn of the argument: God as man.
  • 5:00Mudra “tyāgarāju” C — back to the pallavi, now as a conclusion.
  • 5:20Kalpanaswaras peak Swaras — 1-cycle, then 2-cycle, then long rounds, ending in a korvai that lands on the eduppu.
  • 7:30Final pallavi Close — a short, bright close on Sa.
First listen vs re-listen On a first listen, follow the pallavi coming back and count the sangatis. On a re-listen, follow the G–R–M curl, notice that Dha never appears, and feel the N → P drop on the way down.

12Must-Listen

Pallavi + kalpanaswaras

≈ 4 min · any good live concert recording

Why this one. The whole song and raga in miniature: the off-beat entry, the vakra G-R-M, the bright P-N-Ṡ, and the plea repeated until it really does feel like an argument with yourself.

Recommended. Nedunuri Krishnamurthy for the meaning, then U. Srinivas or A. Kanyakumari for the sparkle.
