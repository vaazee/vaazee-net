In brief

A four-word pallavi, cheḍē buddhi mānurā: “Give up the thinking that ruins you!” Tyagaraja scolds his own mind in Atana, the most commanding, vīra (heroic) raga in the repertoire. The argument: you reap what you deserve, so recognise that Vāsudēva (God as indweller) dwells in you, and meditate on Him. Listen for the bold R–M–P–N–Ṡ leaps going up and the zig-zag P M P G R S coming down.

“śrī vāsudēvas-sarvamanucunu cintiñcarā”
Holding that Vāsudēva is everything, meditate!— Charanam, quoting Bhagavad Gita 7.19

1Identity & Context

An upadēśa (advice) kriti: short, blunt ethics set to a fiery raga.

TitleCheḍē buddhi mānurā (also ceḍē, sometimes chadē). Telugu cheḍu = to be spoiled or ruined, so cheḍē buddhi = “ruinous thinking”.
ComposerTyagaraja (1767–1847), Tiruvaiyaru. One of the Carnatic Trinity; a vāggēyakāra.
Raga · TalaAtana (janya of 29th mela Shankarabharanam) · Adi
Language · DateTelugu · unknown (passed down orally through his disciples)
Kindred kritisBuddhi rādu (Shankarabharanam), Manavyālakiñcarādaṭē (Nalinakanti). Other Tyagaraja Atanas: Ēla nī dayarādu, Anupama guṇāmbudhi.
Tone of address The mind is addressed with the brusque -rā (mānurā, jūḍarā, cintiñcarā). It is being told off, not coaxed. Compare Manavyālakiñcarādaṭē, where the softer, intimate aṭē pleads with the mind instead.

2Formal Structure

Command → challenge → reasoning and answer → command again.

PPallavi“cheḍē buddhi mānurā”1 linerefrain

Four words and a single command. The brevity suits Atana's punchy attack, and the line carries many sangatis that get quicker and sharper in their turns. This is the standard spot for swaras.

AAnupallavi“iḍē pātramevarō jūḍarā”toward tarachallenge

“Look! Who is the real pātram here?” The melody climbs on jūḍarā (“look!”).

CCharanam“bhū-vāsiki tagu phalamu…”4 linesreasoning + answer

Calmer and more reasoning in tone. It sets out the karma argument, quotes the Gita's Vāsudēvaḥ sarvam, instructs “meditate!”, and ends with the mudra tyāgarāja.

3The Raga — Atana

Valour in sound: leaping ascents, a crooked descent and a couple of foreign notes.

Parent 29 Shankarabharanam
Type Audava–vakra sampoorna, bhashanga

Avarohana Ṡ N₃ D₂ P M₁ P G₃ R₂ S

The shape of Atana — toggle to compare

Atana ascent: S R M P N Ṡ. It skips Ga and Dha, so every step from Ri upward is a leap. That lunge toward the upper Sa is Atana's heroic energy.

Atana is not related to the Hindustani raga Adana, despite the similar name.

4Drone & Tonality

Energy from distance and from the snap back to Pa.

Against the tambura's S–P–Ṡ, Atana's ascent lunges (R→M, P→N) instead of stepping, so each climb feels like a charge toward Ṡ. Going down, the P M P hook keeps snapping back to Pa before it lets the line fall to Sa.

Music mirrors meaning The kriti is one long admonition, and the raga behaves like a raised voice and a wagging finger. Atana's vīra rasa turns the rebuke into a call to fight yourself, with inner discipline as the act of courage.

5Rhythm, Tala & Tempo

  • Adi tala: 8 beats (laghu of 4 + two drutams of 2).
  • Kalam: usually medium, with crisp sangatis. One reviewer says it “requires real quick turns”.
  • The outlier: M.D. Ramanathan's famously slow kālapramāṇam turns the scolding into a weighty meditation.
  • Kalpanaswaram at cheḍē buddhi mānurā, full of leaps and the P-M-P hook, ending in a korvai.

6Ensemble & Texture

A standard kutcheri: voice, violin, mridangam (plus ghatam/kanjira) and the tambura drone. The texture is heterophonic, with the violin shadowing the voice. Atana's attack-heavy phrases make for especially lively violin replies in the swara exchanges.

7Lyrics, Meaning & Emotional Arc

Full text, word-by-word gloss, and the ideas behind it.

Pallavi

cheḍē buddhi mānurā

Give up the thinking that leads to ruin!

Anupallavi

iḍē pātram(e)varō jūḍarā (cheḍē…)

Look and see who the real Master (vessel) here is, and give up ruinous thinking!

Charanam

bhū-vāsiki tagu phalamu kalgun(a)ni
budhulu palka vina lēdā manasā

O mind! Haven't you heard the wise say that every being on earth gets the results it deserves? So, holding that “Śrī Vāsudēva is everything”, meditate on the Lord praised by Tyagaraja, and give up ruinous thinking!

What is “pātram”? Older books read iḍē pātramevarō jūḍarā as advice about charity: “see who is a deserving recipient before giving”. The Thyagaraja Vaibhavam commentator argues that because of iḍē (“here”), pātram is the Indweller, the Self in this body. The charanam's Vāsudēva then answers the anupallavi's question. Both readings circulate; the second makes the song a tight question and answer.
The Gita quotation Vāsudēvaḥ sarvam iti comes straight from Bhagavad Gita 7.19: “At the end of many births the wise one takes refuge in Me, realising that Vāsudēva is all; such a great soul is very rare.” Shankara glosses Vāsudēva (Vishnu Sahasranama #332) as the one who dwells (vasu) in all beings and illumines (deva) them.

What the mapping shows The pallavi's buddhi mānurā (pdN P · pp mg M · P) carries Atana's crooked signature: a jump up to Ni, then P-M-G-M and back up to P. That is the raga's vakra M P G twist, and it sits on the very words that tell the mind to give up its ruinous thinking. The anupallavi's pātram(e)varō leaps from P straight to the upper Ṡ, so the question "who is the master here?" is asked at the top of the range. In the charanam the melody runs one note per syllable, like plain speech, and that is where the argument is made. Two small differences from the printed text: this version sings vāsudēvudē instead of vāsudēvas, and manasā isn't in the charanam notation. Its later pallavi sangatis add manasā after mānurā instead.

9Significance

  • A concert energiser. Short, fiery and swara-friendly, it is often placed in the first half or straight after the main piece.
  • A model of Atana's grammar. The pallavi alone teaches the leap-and-hammer upper register and the vakra descent.
  • Tyagaraja as moral teacher. Alongside Buddhi rādu, it shows his upadēśa side: short, direct ethics rather than narrative bhakti.

10Performance Practice & Recordings

  • Alapana is optional and usually short; Atana's character comes across fast.
  • Neraval, where used, suits śrī vāsudēvas-sarvamanucunu, the line that answers the question.
  • Speed is the key choice. Brisk = scolding and vīra; slow (MDR) = grave and meditative.

11Listening Guide

A typical ~7-minute rendition. Times are approximate.

  • 0:00Short Atana sketch Sketch — R-M-P-N-Ṡ leaps; hammering on Ni and Ṡ; the P M P G R S landing.
  • 1:00Pallavi P — four words, many sangatis, each sharper in its turns.
  • 2:15Anupallavi: “jūḍarā!” challenge A — the climb on “look!”
  • 3:15Charanam: bhū-vāsiki… C — calmer, reasoning, more syllables per beat.
  • 4:00“śrī vāsudēvas-sarvamanucunu” the answer C — the Gita quotation; often the most expansive line.
  • 4:40Mudra → pallavi C — the command comes back, now justified.
  • 5:00Kalpanaswaras peak Swaras — leaps and the P-M-P hook, ending in a korvai.
  • 6:45Final pallavi Close — a crisp close on Sa.
First listen vs re-listen On a first listen, track the four-word pallavi each time it returns. On a re-listen, notice where Ga and Dha are skipped going up and where they come back going down.

12Must-Listen

Pallavi + sangatis + kalpanaswaras

≈ 3–4 min

Why this one. Everything that makes Atana what it is gets packed into four words: leap, hammer, curl, land.

Recommended. D.K. Jayaraman for the classic brisk reading, then M.D. Ramanathan to hear how much changes when the same notes slow down.
