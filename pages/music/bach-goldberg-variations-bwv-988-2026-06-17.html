In brief

The Goldbergs are not variations on a tune — they are thirty variations on the Aria's 32-bar bass and harmonic plan, a fixed ground that hides under every surface. The design is engineered with cathedral precision: ten triptychs (character → virtuoso → canon), a canon ladder whose interval of imitation widens by one step every third variation (unison → ninth), a French Overture raising the curtain at the exact midpoint, and three shadows of G minor placed for maximum weight.

The signature move to listen for: the Aria da Capo at the end — the same notes as the opening, transfigured. The form is turned inside out; instead of the theme being varied, the listener has been.

"The supreme expression of harmonic agony in eighteenth-century music."— Donald Tovey, on Variation 25

1Identity & Context

The capstone of the Clavier-Übung, and a magnificently anachronistic monument at the dusk of the Baroque.

  • Full titleClavier Übung … einer ARIA mit verschiedenen Veränderungen vors Clavicimbal mit 2 Manualen — "Keyboard Practice consisting of an Aria with Diverse Variations for harpsichord with two manuals."
  • ComposerJohann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750), aged 56 — late period.
  • Published1741, Balthasar Schmid of Nuremberg; composed across the late 1730s.
  • PositionPart IV (the capstone) of the Clavier-Übung series — alongside the Art of Fugue, Musical Offering and Mass in B Minor as the encyclopedic monuments of Bach's final decade.

The Goldberg legend

Forkel's 1802 biography tells of Count Keyserlingk, an insomniac Russian ambassador, and his teenage harpsichordist Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, who played the variations through sleepless nights — Bach supposedly rewarded with a gold goblet of 100 louis d'or. Modern scholarship is skeptical: there is no dedication on the title page (unthinkable for a real commission) and Goldberg was only 14 in 1741. The work was almost certainly conceived independently as the climax of the Clavier-Übung, the anecdote a posthumous embellishment.

A hidden signature Bach's personal copy (the "Handexemplar," rediscovered 1974) appends fourteen further canons on the Aria's first eight bass notes (BWV 1087). The number is no accident: B(2)+A(1)+C(3)+H(8) = 14 — Bach's number-alphabet signature. He kept mining those eight notes for years.

Historical moment

1741 sits at the cusp of the late Baroque's collapse into the galant and early Classical styles. Bach's own sons were moving toward lighter textures and periodic phrasing, explicitly rejecting dense counterpoint. The Goldbergs answer that fashion by absorbing it — Variations 13, 19 and 25 are saturated with galant idioms — into a thoroughly contrapuntal universe.

2Macro-architecture

Not a heap of thirty good variations — a deliberately engineered building with load-bearing symmetries.

The theme is the bass, not the tune

The single most important fact: the variations are not on the Aria's melody but on its bass line and 32-bar harmonic plan — a ground in binary form (||: 16 :||: 16 :||). This makes the work a passacaglia/chaconne hybrid, closer to Purcell's grounds and Handel's chaconnes than to the later Mozartian "theme-and-variations." The melody is decorative scaffolding; the real subject is the descending bass.

The ten triptychs and the canon ladder

The 30 variations divide into ten groups of three, each following the same recipe — a character piece, then a brilliant two-manual showpiece, then a canon. And every canon climbs: the interval of imitation widens by one step each time, from the unison (Var. 3) to the ninth (Var. 27). Where a canon at the tenth "should" fall (Var. 30), Bach substitutes a Quodlibet.

Pattern note · Group 10 The final triptych breaks the mold: position 1 (Var. 28) is also a virtuoso showpiece, so two brilliant two-manual variations (28, 29) stack back-to-back before the Quodlibet. Virtuosity peaks just before the homecoming — the building's last flourish before the door closes.

The three shadows of G minor

Of 32 movements, only three turn to G minor — Variations 15, 21, 25 — and their spacing clusters the darkness in the second half, with the deepest (25) falling near the work's golden section. Crucially, Var. 25 is the only minor piece that is not a canon: the one moment Bach lets free expression off the contrapuntal leash.

  • 32 movementsAria + 30 variations + Aria da capo — mirroring the Aria's 32 bars. 32 = 2×16 = 4×8.
  • Every 3rd = canonVars. 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27 — interval 1 through 9.
  • Exact midpointVariation 16, a French Overture, raising the curtain on "Act II."
  • BookendsThe identical Aria opens and closes — a frame around the whole.

3Micro-structure

The footsteps inside the architecture: the disguised bass, the mirror canons, the bassless ninth, the two-meter illusion.

The ground bass, note by note

Strip away the Aria's ornament and the skeleton is a descending bass with a cadential turn — the archetypal Baroque tetrachord plus cadence. Its first eight notes are the cell Bach mined for the fourteen Handexemplar canons:

First half  (toward D):  G  F♯  E  D │ B  C  D  G  → cadence in D major
Second half (homeward):  drifts flatward (E min · C · A) → strong G-major close

Harmonic skeleton:
 ||:  G   D   e   b   C   G   a   D  :||    (bars 1–16, out to the dominant)
 ||:  G⁷  C   F   d   g   C   D   G  :||    (bars 17–32, flatward, then home)

This 32-bar plan is preserved under every variation. Var. 1 and Var. 25 share exactly this frame — and sound like different planets. That is the central magic: thirty surfaces, one foundation.

How a canon actually works here — and how Bach raises the stakes

Below: the same canonic line, first in similar motion (the answer copies the leader's contour exactly, a fixed interval away — Vars. 3, 6, 9, 18, 21, 24) and then in contrary motion (the answer is the leader turned upside-down — the rare inverted canons of Vars. 12 and 15). Toggle to see the difference.

Similar motion: the follower (gold) traces the leader's exact contour, displaced by the canon's interval. Used in Variations 3, 6, 9, 18, 21, 24 and the bassless 27.

Beneath the two canonic voices, eight of the nine canons add a free third voice — the bass — that keeps the harmony moving. The lone exception is Variation 27, the canon at the ninth: pure two-voice canon, no bass at all. Having stretched the interval to its widest, Bach removes the safety net and lets the two lines hang in space.

The two-meter illusion · Var. 26 One hand plays 18/16 against the other's 3/4, both in the same system. The math is exact (18 sixteenths = 3 beats), but the ear hears two simultaneous musics — a glittering perpetual-motion arabesque pouring over a stately sarabande. Bach drops it in right after the catastrophe of Var. 25: devastation answered by dazzle.

4Harmony & Tonality

Diatonic G major as a frame — and a creative cage that makes the wonder possible.

Within a fundamentally functional G major, Bach deploys nearly every device available in 1741: chromatic lament descents (Vars. 15, 21, 25), modal/phrygian colorings and galant sighs (25), secondary dominants tonicizing neighbor keys, dense suspensions and appoggiature in the slow variations, and even rare augmented-sixth and Neapolitan harmonies in Var. 25.

The fixed 32-bar plan is a creative cage, and the awe comes from how invisibly Bach moves inside it. The canonic constraint compounds the difficulty geometrically: a strict canon that also fits the ground and the rising-interval scheme and makes good keyboard music — nine times over.

The da capo recast The returning Aria reframes everything as a frame: the door opens, we walk through thirty rooms, the door closes — and the once-decorative Aria now sounds like an elegy, because the listener has changed.

5Rhythm & Meter

An encyclopedia of Baroque meters and genres — over a nearly blank tempo canvas.

Across 32 movements Bach runs through almost the whole metric vocabulary — 3/4 · 2/4 · 4/4 · 3/8 · 6/8 · 9/8 · 12/8 · 12/16 · 18/16 · 2/2 — many variations existing partly to showcase a meter (the gigue's 6/8 in Var. 7, the pastoral 9/8 in Var. 24, the polymetric 18/16-over-3/4 in Var. 26). The set doubles as a genre catalogue: polonaise, invention, passepied, gigue, fughetta, aria, French overture, alla breve motet, toccata, sarabande and quodlibet.

A near-blank canvas Bach left almost no tempo markings — only Var. 7 (al tempo di Giga), Var. 15 (Andante), Var. 25 (Adagio) and the implicit pacing of the Overture. Everything else is inferred from genre and figuration — which is why "tempo plans" diverge wildly (Gould's 1981 is roughly twice as slow as his 1955).

6Texture & Timbre

The two-manual requirement is structural, not decorative — and the harpsichord's terraced palette is built into the design.

Variations 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 26, 28, 29 are marked a 2 Clav. because the hands cross continuously and would physically collide on one keyboard — a real argument for hearing the work on harpsichord at least once. On a modern piano these become genuine puzzles of fingering and voicing.

The textural catalogue: two-voice invention (1, 2, 4); two-voice canon + free bass (3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 21, 24); bassless two-voice canon (27); four-voice fughetta (10) and alla-breve motet (22); French overture with fugal allegro (16); brilliant hand-crossing toccatas (5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23, 28, 29); aria cantilena (13); and quodlibet polyphony (30).

On a two-manual instrument with 8′ and 4′ stops, Bach registers for contrast — bright (manuals coupled, 4′ adding a brilliant octave) for showpieces, soft (single 8′) for canons and meditations. No crescendo is possible, but the two keyboards enable terraced dynamics, and Bach builds his structural contrasts on exactly that.

7Emotional Arc & Dramaturgy

A genuine dramatic trajectory with two climaxes, not a flat sequence.

  • Aria — a private, contemplative invitation. A door opens.
  • Vars. 1–15 — gathering brilliance and intimacy; Var. 13 the first deep lyricism; Var. 15 (g minor) closes the first half on a hanging fifth.
  • Var. 16 — French Overture; Act II begins with pomp.
  • Vars. 17–24 — denser, more adventurous; Var. 21 (g minor) darkens; Var. 22 a still clearing.
  • Var. 25 — the "Black Pearl," the emotional nadir; time stops.
  • Vars. 26–29 — rebirth into radiant G major; arabesques, trills, a chordal toccata.
  • Var. 30 — Quodlibet; folk songs, family, laughter.
  • Aria da Capo — the same notes, transfigured.
The masterstroke Hearing the identical Aria again — same notes, ornaments, harmony — yet heavier and more self-aware. The variation form is turned inside out: instead of the theme being varied, the listener has been varied.

8Historical Significance & Influence

From 1741 curiosity to the supreme keyboard variation set in Western music.

In 1741 the Goldbergs were a connoisseur's curiosity — engraved, expensive, immensely difficult, out of step with galant fashion. Bach's reputation eclipsed after 1750 and the work stayed a specialist's monument through the 18th century. Beethoven knew it and consulted it while writing the Diabelli Variations (1819–23), his explicit attempt to out-encyclopedia Bach.

Wanda Landowska (1933) restored it to the living repertoire on harpsichord and named Var. 25 the "Black Pearl." Glenn Gould (1955) then made it iconic — a 38-minute, dazzlingly clear LP that welded the Goldbergs to the idea of intellectual depth. His 1981 re-recording, slower and interior, closed his life-arc with the work; he died nine days after its release.

Its influence runs through Beethoven's Diabelli, Brahms's Handel and Paganini variations, and onward to Webern, Busoni, Rzewski and the arrangers (Sitkovetsky's string trio). It has become culture too — appearing in The Silence of the Lambs, The English Patient, and Thomas Bernhard's The Loser. With The Art of Fugue, the Mass in B Minor and the Well-Tempered Clavier, it is one of the four high monuments of Bach's output.

10Listening Guide

Timings based on a ~75-minute performance with most repeats. For Gould 1955 (38 min), roughly halve them. Filter by half.

  • 0:00Aria I — feel the bass under the ornaments; sarabande weight on beat 2.
  • ~4:00Var. 1 I — first surge; buoyant two-voice invention, hemiola.
  • ~8:00Var. 3 — Canon at unison I — voices imitate one bar apart over a free bass.
  • ~10:00Var. 5 I — first two-manual fireworks; hand-crossing.
  • ~20:00Var. 10 — Fughetta I — a real four-voice fugue; pivot of the first half.
  • ~25:00Var. 13 I — first deep lyricism; galant aria over arpeggios.
  • ~30:00Var. 15 — Canon at 5th (g, inverted) hinge I — first minor; mirror canon; ends on a hanging rising fifth.
  • ~33:00Var. 16 — French Overture midpoint II — dotted grave + fugal allegro; Act II begins.
  • ~43:00Var. 21 — Canon at 7th (g) II — chromatic, mournful; second minor.
  • ~46:00Var. 22 — Alla breve II — a still, motet-like clearing.
  • ~54:00Var. 25 — "Black Pearl" (g, Adagio) peak II — chromatic descents, sigh figures; longest variation.
  • ~63:00Var. 26 II — rebirth; polymetric — fast 18/16 over slow 3/4.
  • ~66:00Var. 27 — Canon at 9th II — widest canon, no bass; two lines hanging in space.
  • ~69:00Var. 29 peak II — chordal toccata; the kinetic peak.
  • ~72:00Var. 30 — Quodlibet II — two folk tunes braided over the ground; humanity returns.
  • ~75:00Aria da Capo II — same notes; you are not the same listener.

First-listen focus

Don't track all thirty. Listen for: (1) the Aria's character; (2) Var. 15's hanging fifth; (3) Var. 25's chromatic anguish; (4) Var. 30's two braided folk tunes; (5) the Aria da Capo — and ask why it now feels different.

11Must-Listen Tracks

If you have fifteen minutes: the door and the heart.

Aria (opening)

≈ 3–5 min · the door of the whole work

Why this one. The entire 80-minute journey is encoded here — the bass you'll hear in thirty guises, the harmonic plan, the emotional restraint. It is also one of the most beautiful single pieces of keyboard music ever written.

Recommended recording. Pierre Hantaï (Mirare, 2003) on harpsichord, or Murray Perahia (Sony, 2000) on piano.

Variation 25 — the "Black Pearl"

≈ 6–10 min · the heart of the work

Why this one. Bach's deepest moment of harmonic anguish in any keyboard work — chromatic, suspended, weeping — proof that the late Baroque could reach expressive depths the Romantics liked to claim as their invention.

Recommended recording. Glenn Gould (CBS, 1981) — glacial and interior, or Víkingur Ólafsson (DG, 2023) — architecturally lit, no less devastating.

If you can spare a third, add Variation 26 (Pinnock or Hantaï) — the polymetric rebirth into G major right after the Var. 25 catastrophe. The juxtaposition is one of the most thrilling in the repertoire.
