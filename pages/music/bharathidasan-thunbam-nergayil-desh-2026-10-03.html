In brief

“When sorrow comes, won't you take up the yāzh and bring me joy?” A parent asks a child to heal a harsh world with the Tamil arts: music, then dance, then the ethics of the Kural and the poetry of the Sangam age. Every stanza ends on the same gentle question, māṭṭāyā? (“won't you?”). Dandapani Desikar found its voice in Desh, a raga of longing borrowed from Hindustani music. Listen for the open rise R M P N Ṡ and the soft fall M G R, G S that closes each plea.

“tamizhc selvam āka māṭṭāyā?”
Won't you become Tamil's treasure?— the last line of the second charanam

1Identity & Context

A Dravidian-movement poem, a Tamil Isai musician's tune and a 1951 film: three histories meet in one song.

TitleTunbam nērgaiyil (also Thunbam nergayil): “when sorrow comes”. Often cited with the next words, yāzh eḍuttu (“taking up the yāzh”).
WordsBharathidasan (K. Subburathinam, Puducherry 1891 – Madras 1964). He named himself “servant of Bharati” after Subramania Bharati and became the leading poet of the Self-Respect / Dravidian movement.
TuneM. M. Dandapani Desikar (1908–1972), “Isai Arasu”: Odhuvar-trained singer, star of the saint films (Nandanar, 1942), for fifteen years head of music at Annamalai University. He set the poem in Desh after two years of deliberation.
Raga · TalaDesh (janya of 28th mela Harikambhoji) · Chatusra Eka (some lists give Adi)
FilmAVM's Ōr Iravu (11 April 1951; dir. P. Neelakantan, from C. N. Annadurai's play; music R. Sudarsanam, “based on a composition by Dandapani Desikar”). Sung by M. S. Rajeswari & V. J. Varma; on screen, Lalitha of the Travancore Sisters with A. Nageswara Rao. The film didn't do well; the song did.
MudraNone. Bharathidasan didn't sign his poems inside the text the way the Trinity did.
The Atana demonstration In a rare speech from the concert platform, Desikar said a raga has to be chosen for the sentiment of the words, and sang the opening of Tunbam nērgaiyil in Atana to show how wrong a mismatch sounds. Atana is the heroic raga of Cheḍē Buddhi Mānurā, exactly the wrong colour for a tender plea. Bharathidasan was charmed by the Desh setting, and Desikar went on to set more of his poems.
The moment The 1940s Tamil Isai movement (Tamil Isai Sangam, 1943) pressed for Tamil songs on a concert stage dominated by Telugu and Sanskrit kritis, and Desikar was one of its main voices. A poem that asks for Tamil song, Tamil dance and Tamil literature as the cure for sorrow reads like that movement's manifesto, set to music.

2Formal Structure

Four stanzas, one build, and a walk through the “three Tamils”.

The poem is laid out like a kriti, but every section has the same three-couplet build: a long opening line, then a short line ending in a question that is sung twice, then the request and the closing question, twice again.

PPallavi“tunbam nērgaiyil yāzh eḍuttu nī”isairefrain

Sorrow, the yāzh, a heart without love, a song in Tamil. The two questions: inbam sērkka māṭṭāyā? (won't you bring joy?) and allal nīkka māṭṭāyā? (won't you take the pain away?).

AAnupallavi“vanbum eḷimaiyum sūzhum nāṭṭilē”nāṭakamthe wider view

The sorrow turns social: a land closed in by cruelty and helplessness. The cure: the old Tamil kūttu, danced. āḍik kāṭṭa māṭṭāyā?

C1Charanam 1“aṟam iteṉṟum yām maṟam iteṉṟumē”iyalgrave

When we can't tell right from wrong, recite one word of the Tirukkuṟaḷ. Some singers actually insert the couplet (Kural 138).

C2Charanam 2“puṟam iteṉṟum nal akam iteṉṟumē”iyalclimax

The Sangam poets' “outer” and “inner” worlds; then the parent's real hope: uṉai īṉṟa em uyirc selvam āka māṭṭāyā? “Won't you become the treasure of our life, we who bore you?”

The run-in words

Each repeated line is linked by a word printed at the end of the line before and sung as a run-in. In the pallavi, emakkinbam (“joy for us”) is even split across the break: emak- / -kinbam.

3The Raga — Desh

A Hindustani raga of longing, with two Nis: sharp on the way up, flat on the way down.

Parent 28 Harikambhoji
Type Audava–sampoorna, vakra, bhashanga
Arohana S R₂ M₁ P N₃ Ṡ
Avarohana Ṡ N₂ D₂ P M₁ G₃ R₂ G₃ S
Pakad R, M P N Ṡ, Ṙ N₂ D P, M G R, G N̤ S
Mood longing (viraha); night, monsoon

Desh or Atana? Desikar's test, as a toggle

Ascent, Desh and Atana alike: S R M P N Ṡ. Both skip Ga and Dha and push through sharp Ni to the upper Sa. That's why the words fit either raga note for note.

4Drone & Tonality

Hopeful rises, soft landings.

Against the tambura's S–P–Ṡ, Desh's rises are open and hopeful: with no Ga or Dha in the way, the line goes straight for the upper Sa. Its falls are soft. The flat Ni and the R G S curl keep each phrase from landing hard.

Music mirrors meaning That is the shape of the text too: a hopeful request, then a gentle, open question. Māṭṭāyā? isn't an order; it's a coaxing “won't you?”, and Desh lets it trail off like one.

5Rhythm, Tala & Tempo

  • Chatusra Eka (4 beats) in P. R. Ramachander's and the Thamizh Isai listings; Adi (8 beats) in others. Either way the song moves in an easy four-beat lilt.
  • Tempo: medium and unhurried. This is a song for the words.
  • Improvisation: usually little or none. No kalpanaswaras; sometimes a short Desh alapana or a Tamil viruttam before it.

6Ensemble & Texture

In concert: voice, violin, mridangam and tambura, in heterophony with the violin shadowing the voice. The 1951 film version was a duet for female and male voice with R. Sudarsanam's studio orchestra. It also travels well without words: there are veena (Rajesh Vaidhya) and nagaswaram (MPN Sethuraman & Ponnuswamy, with vocalist Manikka Vinayagam) versions.

Who is speaking? uṉai īṉṟa em, “we who bore you”, settles it: a parent talks to a child. The tenderness recalls Subramania Bharati's Chinnañ ciṟu kiḷiyē, a parent's song to a little daughter, by the poet Bharathidasan named himself after.
The Kural, actually sung Some versions (P. R. Ramachander's text) insert a couplet into charanam 1: naṉṟikku vittāgum nallozhukkam; tīyozhukkam eṉṟum iḍumbai tarum. “Good conduct is the seed of good; bad conduct brings sorrow, always” (Kural 138). The “one word of the Kural” arrives on cue.

Art as medicine. The sorrow is social, not just private: a land of vanbu (cruelty) and eḷimai (helplessness), hearts emptied of love. The cure is culture, and specifically Tamil culture. That was the point of both Bharathidasan's politics and the Tamil Isai movement.

8Significance

  • One of the best-loved Tamil songs in the Carnatic repertoire, a fixture of the lighter, post-main part of a kutcheri.
  • A landmark of the Tamil Isai movement: a modern, secular poem by a Dravidian-movement poet, made concert-worthy by a Tamil Isai musician's careful raga choice.
  • A lesson in raga choice. Desikar's Atana demonstration makes it a textbook case of matching rasa to words.
  • Film-to-concert crossover. It outlived a 1951 flop to move freely between cinema, kutcheri, nagaswaram and fusion.
  • Desh at home in the South. Alongside Lalgudi Jayaraman's Desh tillana, it's one of the pieces that made this Hindustani import feel native to Carnatic concerts.

9Performance Practice & Recordings

  • Opening: straight in, or after a short Desh alapana or a Tamil viruttam.
  • Text choices: both charanams or one; whether to insert the Kural couplet; nīkka or tīrkka.
  • The real test: the repeated māṭṭāyā? lines. The best versions shade each repeat differently instead of just singing it twice.

10Listening Guide

A typical ~6-minute concert rendition. Times are approximate.

  • 0:00Desh sketch or viruttam Sketch — the open R M P N Ṡ and the soft fall through flat Ni.
  • 0:45“tunbam nērgaiyil yāzh eḍuttu nī” P — then the first māṭṭāyā? and its repeat via emak- / -kinbam.
  • 1:30“anbilā neñjil tamizhil pāḍi nī” P — the second couplet; kaṇṇē leads into the last repeat.
  • 2:15“vanbum eḷimaiyum…” widest A — the song opens out, as anupallavis do; āḍik kāṭṭa māṭṭāyā?
  • 3:15Pallavi returns P
  • 3:45“aṟam iteṉṟum…” C1 — graver; listen for whether the singer inserts the Kural couplet.
  • 4:45“tamizhc selvam āka māṭṭāyā?” climax C2 — the parent's real hope.
  • 5:30Pallavi and close P — the R G S curl, home on Sa.
First listen vs re-listen On a first listen, count the māṭṭāyā? questions and notice how each one is softened at its end. On a re-listen, follow the two Nis: sharp on the way up, flat on the way down.

11Must-Listen

The pallavi, in Dandapani Desikar's own voice

≈ 2 min

Why this one. It's the source: the first line, the coaxing question, and the soft Desh curl that answers it, sung by the man who spent two years choosing the raga.

Recommended. M. M. Dandapani Desikar first, then P. Unnikrishnan to hear how a modern voice inherited it.
