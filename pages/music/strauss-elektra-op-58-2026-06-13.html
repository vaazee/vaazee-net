In brief

Elektra is the sound of tonality at the breaking point. In one unbroken 100-minute act, Strauss drives post-Wagnerian chromaticism into bitonality and screaming dissonance — two keys sounded at once — to anatomize a woman who has fused her entire self to a single act of vengeance and cannot survive its completion.

The signature move: he withholds nearly all consonance for two hours, then spends it twice — the radiant recognition scene and the deranged dance of triumph that kills its heroine. Written in 1909, the same year Schoenberg let go of tonality entirely. Strauss peered over the same abyss — and, with Rosenkavalier next, turned back.

"I have penetrated to the uttermost limits of harmony, psychological polyphony — Klytämnestra's dream — and of the receptivity of modern ears."— Richard Strauss, on Elektra

1Identity & Context

The apex of Strauss's modernism — and the first spark of his greatest partnership.

  • Full titleElektra, Tragödie in einem Aufzug, Op. 58 (TrV 223)
  • ComposerRichard Strauss (1864–1949)
  • LibrettistHugo von Hofmannsthal, after his own 1903 play (freely after Sophocles)
  • Composed1906–1908
  • Premiere25 January 1909, Königliches Opernhaus (Semperoper), Dresden — Ernst von Schuch conducting; Annie Krull in the title role
  • Forces~111–112 players — the largest orchestra Strauss ever wrote for in an opera
  • Duration~100–110 minutes, no interval

Where it sits in the output

Elektra is Strauss's second mature opera after Salome (1905), and the first of his great collaborations with Hofmannsthal — the partnership that would yield Der Rosenkavalier, Ariadne auf Naxos, Die Frau ohne Schatten and Arabella. It is the high-water mark of his "modernist" phase. The very next opera, Rosenkavalier (1911), is a sunlit Viennese comedy in waltz-time — one of the most discussed about-faces in music history.

Historical moment 1909 is a hinge in Western music. Schoenberg's monodrama Erwartung — a study of a woman's hysteria, freely atonal — was composed the very year Elektra premiered. Freud's Vienna hangs over the libretto: Klytämnestra's nightmare-monologue is essentially a case study set to music.

Source & adaptation

Sophocles supplies the skeleton; Hofmannsthal supplies the fever. He compresses the action into a single unbroken arc in the bloodstained courtyard of Mycenae, strips away the consolations, and turns the Greek chorus inward into Elektra's own divided psyche. Strauss set the play almost as a Literaturoper — composing directly onto the spoken text with relatively few cuts.

2Formal Structure

No numbers, no arias that stop the clock, no curtain until the heroine falls dead — one gigantic sonata-drama whose "themes" are characters.

Elektra is through-composed: a single continuous span. But the drama articulates itself into a chain of confrontations — duologues, each raising the temperature — framed by an orchestral prologue and the closing dance.

Macro-architecture One long accelerando of dread toward two release-points — the recognition scene (emotional release) and the final dance (kinetic release). The Agamemnon motif frames the whole: the first thing you hear, and it pounds through the final bars. The entire opera is one act of summoning the dead father and discharging the debt owed to him.

4Harmony & Tonality

The chapter that made Elektra notorious — bitonality and emancipated dissonance, just short of Schoenberg's leap.

The "Elektra chord"

The opera's harmonic signature is a celebrated polychord: two major triads superimposed, sounding two keys at once. Its precise analysis is genuinely contested among theorists — which is the point. It is designed to resist tonal explanation, functioning as a sound-object of horror and guilt. The keyboard below shows the principle — two triads a tritone apart, sharing no notes (here B♭ major against E major) — the kind of collision that gives the chord its acrid, unresolvable bite.

Bitonal symbolism

Keys carry meaning, and their collision is the drama:

  • D minor = Agamemnon, death, the blood-debt — the framing key.
  • E♭ / B♭ major = longing, memory, transcendence — Chrysothemis's lyricism and the recognition scene gravitate here.
  • Klytämnestra's "dream" harmony = Strauss's high-water mark of emancipated dissonance: chords that slither without functional bass, tonality dissolving to depict a guilty mind that cannot sleep.
Cadence & closure The opera resists cadence for most of its length, then drives to a blazing, hammered affirmation in the final dance — a tonal arrival wrenched out of two hours of instability, the Agamemnon motif pounding beneath. The last bars are famously, thrillingly violent.

5Rhythm & Tempo

Rhythm as branding; tempo as terror.

  • Rhythmic identity. Motifs are recognized as much by rhythm as pitch — the four hammer-blows of "Agamemnon" are a rhythmic signature first.
  • The dance. The whole opera points toward Elektra's final dance of triumph — a whirling, accelerating, waltz-derived but deranged kinetic frenzy, a maenadic Totentanz. The future Rosenkavalier waltz-king is already audible, here turned demonic.
  • Tempo as terror. Sudden accelerations and grinding, slow "dragging" chordal passages (Klytämnestra's approach) enact psychological states physically. The pacing lurches by design.
  • Metric instability mirrors the unstable harmony — phrase lengths stretch and compress with the characters' breathing.

6Orchestration, Texture & Timbre

An orchestra of 111 used as a psychological scalpel — not mere bulk.

  • Forces (~111 players): quadruple-plus woodwinds including heckelphone (baritone oboe), E♭ clarinet, basset horns and bass clarinet; eight horns (four doubling Wagner tubas), heavy brass; two harps; huge percussion; and an enormous, frequently divided string section — Strauss splits the violins into multiple groups for shimmering, many-voiced textures.
  • Timbral dramaturgy. Colors become psychological masks: snarling low woodwinds and muted brass for Klytämnestra's guilt; gleaming high divided strings and harps for the recognition; raw unison ferocity for the Agamemnon motif.
  • Dynamic architecture. From near-inaudible shudders to overwhelming tuttis. The recognition's hush and the dance's blaze are the two poles.
  • Transparency despite size. Textures are often chamber-like; solo lines emerge from the mass. The full orchestra is unleashed only at the structural climaxes — which is why they land like hammer-blows.

7Emotional Arc & Dramaturgy

A one-act study in obsession, trauma, and catharsis-as-annihilation.

Hofmannsthal's psychology — steeped in the Vienna of Freud — makes Elektra a case study: a woman who has fused her entire identity to a single act of revenge, and who literally cannot survive its completion.

  • Trajectory: isolation → invocation of the dead → failed appeals to the living → false despair → recognition (the one release of tenderness) → the deed → ecstatic dissolution and death.
  • Twin climaxes. Strauss withholds nearly all consonance for two hours, then spends it twice — the recognition (the heart's release) and the dance (the body's release). The genius: catharsis kills Elektra. Having lived only for vengeance, she has no self left once it is achieved.
  • Klytämnestra is no cartoon villain but a terrified, guilt-poisoned insomniac — the dramaturgical center of gravity.
Music as psychology Bitonality = a mind split between past and present. "Dragging" chords = the weight of guilt. The maenad dance = mania discharging into death. The score doesn't accompany the psychology — it is the psychology.

8Historical Significance & Influence

The climax — and the terminus — of Strauss's modernism.

  • What was radical: functional tonality pushed to its breaking point — sustained bitonality and emancipated dissonance in the service of raw psychological violence. With Salome, it defined operatic Expressionism before the term existed.
  • The pivot. Strauss could have gone where Schoenberg went; instead Rosenkavalier (1911) returned to sumptuous tonality. A road taken to its end, then deliberately abandoned.
  • Reception. Premiere audiences and critics were shocked — widely called the most dissonant, brutal stage music yet written. Its reputation has only risen.
  • Influence. Its psychological one-act intensity fed directly into Alban Berg's Wozzeck and the Second Viennese theater of the mind.
  • Place in the canon: the supreme test-piece for dramatic sopranos, and a cornerstone of the modern repertory.

9Performance Practice & Recordings

One of the most punishing roles in opera — a hochdramatischer Sopran riding a colossal orchestra for 100 minutes without leaving the stage.

Conductors balance two ideals: ferocious sonic spectacle versus transparent clarity so the voices survive. Tempo in the dance and the pacing of the Klytämnestra scene define an interpretation.

10Listening Guide

Filter the timeline by scene. Timings keyed to Solti/VPO (~107 min).

  • 0:00Agamemnon motif open — smashed out in unison (D–F–A), no overture. The whole argument in four hammer-blows.
  • 0:02The maids open — gossip about Elektra; one defends her and is dragged off. Hear the orchestra characterize each servant.
  • 0:09Elektra's monologue open — "Allein! Weh, ganz allein." She summons her father and pre-enacts the triumph-dance.
  • 0:19Chrysothemis enters open — the music warms and sings — the first diatonic relief.
  • 0:29Klytämnestra approaches Kly — over "dragging," guilt-laden chords. The bitonal abyss begins.
  • ~0:40The nightmare-monologue abyss Kly — the harmonic heart of darkness; "psychological polyphony."
  • ~0:50The prophecy & the laugh Kly — Elektra's coded death-prophecy and her terrifying laughter as the queen flees.
  • 0:53False news Kly — Orest is reported dead. Elektra turns to Chrysothemis to do the deed; the sister recoils.
  • 1:09Recognition scene keystone rec — the stranger reveals himself. "Orest!" The orchestra blooms into radiant warmth — the emotional summit.
  • 1:29The murders fin — Klytämnestra's two offstage screams. Then Aegisth.
  • 1:39Dance of triumph peak fin — accelerating to frenzy; Elektra collapses dead as the Agamemnon motif hammers the final bars.

First listen: ride the two release-points (recognition, dance). Deeper listen: track the Agamemnon motif's transformations and the bitonal collisions in the Klytämnestra scene.

11Must-Listen

If you have fifteen minutes with the opera, spend them here.

The Recognition Scene — "Orest! Orest!"

≈ from 1:09 in Solti · ~12 min

Why this one. In an opera built almost entirely from dissonance and dread, this is the single passage where Strauss opens the windows: chromatic tension resolves into glowing, sustained diatonic warmth as Elektra recognizes her brother and her hatred dissolves, for a few minutes, into tenderness. It is the structural and emotional keystone — the consonance the whole work has been hoarding — and it proves everything harsh elsewhere was a deliberate withholding. The most beautiful music Strauss wrote before Rosenkavalier, and unbearably moving in context.

Recommended recording. Solti / Vienna Philharmonic — Birgit Nilsson & Tom Krause (Decca, 1966–67) Nilsson floats the line over Solti's shimmering Vienna strings without a hint of strain. For danger over polish, Mitropoulos (Orfeo, 1957) is the harrowing alternative.
